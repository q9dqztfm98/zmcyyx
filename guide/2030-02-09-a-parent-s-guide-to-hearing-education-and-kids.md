# A Parent’s Guide to Hearing, Education and Kids

Hearing, Education, and Kids: A Parent’s Guide

Key takeaways

Nearly 3 out of every 1,000 children in the United States are born with some degree of hearing loss

Hearing helps children to recognize and learn speech, form words, and understand their surroundings

Resources and devices help children navigate hearing loss in social and academic environments

Hearing is essential for people to learn about the world around them. But according to the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders, nearly 3 out of every 1,000 children in the United States are born with some degree of hearing loss. Additionally, more than 90 percent of deaf children have hearing parents. Misconceptions and misunderstandings about hearing loss can unintentionally limit the potential of affected children. If you have a child experiencing hearing loss, accessing resources and learning about how hearing affects development is critical to properly support your child’s social, academic, and developmental success.

Hearing and Development

In the early years, hearing allows babies and children to process what’s happening in their surroundings during childhood development. It also helps them model sounds for speech development and pick up on nonverbal cues from others to develop social awareness.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association reports areas of development and learning that depend heavily on hearing in the below list.

Learning to Use Words and Sentences Children with hearing loss can have difficulties hearing and understanding language. They may need help with subtleties, like past tense, plurals, or complex sentences. Often, they tend to use shorter sentences or eliminate words like “the,” “an,” and “are,” which can make their speech sound significantly different than that of their peers. They may also have trouble differentiating words with multiple meanings.

Speech Development Hearing loss can affect the clarity of a child’s speech, as they may not hear the sounds they’re making as well as others can. They may not effectively regulate the volume and pitch of their voices, sometimes speaking noticeably louder or softer than others. Sounds like “s,” “sh,” “f,” “t,” and “k” are quieter than others, making them more difficult to hear, so children with hearing loss may not use these sounds.

Achieving Academic Success Hearing loss without necessary accommodations may cause problems in school, especially in reading, writing, or math. Children with hearing loss may fall one to four grades behind, even at the risk of not progressing past a third- or fourth-grade level. As their peers progress, falling behind will become more and more noticeable. It’s essential to maximize school support as soon as possible.

Developing Social Skills Difficulty communicating or being understood by peers can cause problems with socializing or making friends. Children may not understand hearing loss and think people with hearing loss sound weird. Stress or anxiety related to difficulty learning may make children with hearing loss less friendly in school and less likely to attempt new friendships. Many feel alone and withdraw from play and learning interaction.

Causes of Hearing Loss in Children

Before addressing the developmental aspects of hearing loss, it’s important to understand the different types of hearing loss that affect children. According to the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, hearing loss can develop from congenital causes (present at birth), and acquired causes (occurring after birth).

Congenital Causes More than half of congenital hearing loss cases may result from genetic factors. If either parent carries a dominant gene for hearing loss or both parents carry a recessive gene, they risk passing hearing loss onto a child. In addition to dominant and recessive genes, sex-linked and mitochondrial genes can also cause congenital hearing loss.

Recessive genetic hearing loss is typically more surprising, as neither parent may have hearing loss themselves or in their families. Yet parents may anticipate their child developing hearing loss if they have a dominant gene, as they may have hearing loss themselves.

Hearing loss may also be a characteristic of another genetic syndrome present at birth, like Down syndrome or Treacher-Collins syndrome. Hearing loss is secondary to these conditions — as hearing loss may accompany this condition but not always.

Non-Congenital Causes Birth circumstances, such as premature delivery or lack of oxygen to the brain during or after delivery, can cause hearing loss, as can illnesses like chicken pox, mumps, or meningitis, a head injury, or damaging noise exposure levels.

The most common cause of hearing loss in young children is a non-congenital condition called otitis media, which is commonly known as an ear infection. Otitis media is inflammation in the middle ear that may also lead to an infection. Most children will experience otitis media at least once before turning three. Mild cases can cause a slight decrease in hearing that resolves once inflammation is relieved, while severe and repeated cases can result in permanent hearing loss.

Signs of Auditory Processing Difficulty

Children often can’t explain their hearing difficulty, so it's helpful to understand signs that may indicate hearing loss, as it can occur in various levels, from mild to severe, and symptoms can be nuanced. In addition to hearing loss, children may experience auditory processing difficulty, meaning they hear sounds but can’t fully process them.

According to Child Mind Institute and Johns Hopkins Medicine, signs of auditory processing difficulty may present in the following ways below.

Difficulty Paying Attention or Concentrating Children who have trouble hearing may disconnect easily from conversations or social situations. Parents or educators may assume the child has a short attention span or ADHD, leading them to disengage. Instead, the child’s difficulty hearing and the excess effort required to engage causes them to withdraw.

Delayed Responses or Actions A delayed reaction to questions or instructions may sometimes seem like a lack of interest or intellectual capacity. For children with hearing loss, the delay results from needing extra time to process what they are hearing.

Covering Their Ears or Avoiding Noise Hypersensitivity may also accompany hearing difficulties, and children with hypersensitivity to noise can have similar challenges to those with hearing loss. Too much noise, or noises that are too loud, can make it hard for children with hearing hypersensitivity to focus on specific sounds like conversations or instructions.

Fluctuating Hearing Loss Children with auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder may experience hearing loss that fluctuates daily, or even within hours. Additionally, hearing and understanding speech amidst background noise is increasingly difficult.

Asking for Repetition Children with hearing loss may have trouble hearing what is said to them and will often ask for things to be repeated or say “what?” frequently in place of a response.

Speaking in a Monotone or Flat Voice Hearing loss means that affected children can’t hear their voices well when they speak. As a result, mimicking the tones they hear when learning speech through interaction becomes more challenging, resulting in a noticeably monotone voice.

Signs of Hearing Loss in Babies vs. Children

Since babies and older children are at different stages of development, their signs of hearing loss will vary considerably.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, babies with hearing loss may not startle at loud noises or turn and focus in the direction of sounds. Additionally, hearing loss can delay standard speech milestones, like saying “mama” and “dada,” around one year old. Parents may notice that babies turn their heads when they see someone, but maybe not if someone speaks out of sight.

Older children will likely display more noticeable, although not always obvious, signs of hearing loss. Indications a child may have hearing issues are delayed or unclear speech, difficulty following directions or answering questions, frequent requests for repetition, trouble hearing in noisy environments, and turning the volume too high if they can control devices like tablets, TVs, or music players.

Other Ways Hearing Impacts a Child’s Learning

Hearing is integral to child development, and hearing loss isn’t the only hearing-related condition that can complicate development and education. Two other hearing conditions that present in children are hyperacusis and overstimulation.

Hyperacusis is sensitivity to tones, sounds, and volumes others find tolerable. It can cause children to try to tune out sounds that they find unpleasant, painful, or intense. Hyperacusis often results in anxiety in children, especially in social or educational settings, leading the child to avoid specific lessons, games, or other children that overwhelm their sensitive hearing.

Protecting Your Child’s Hearing Health

Attend well visits and screenings as scheduled. Your child’s doctor may pick up on signs of hearing loss during routine appointments and recommend appropriate next steps.

Take medication for ear infections as prescribed. If your child has an ear infection and is prescribed medication, it’s important to take the medication as ordered to ensure the infection is fully treated and less likely to cause permanent hearing damage.

Monitor the volume on your child’s devices. Always be mindful of the volume on tablets, headphones, and the TV to avoid damage from excessive volume exposure. Headphones can be particularly dangerous for small children’s hearing because the volume can be far too high without anyone noticing.

Wear hearing protection around loud noises. Damaging noise levels can come from music, power tools, lawnmowers, fireworks — things that children may hear regularly.

Address possible signs of hearing loss or sensitivity as soon as possible. Noticing signs of hearing loss in your child and getting hearing care quickly can keep hearing issues from progressing and negatively affecting education and development.

Model good hearing-related behavior. Setting an example like listening to sounds at a safe volume and always wearing hearing protection when indicated.

A Note About Noise Exposure

Dangerous noise levels aren’t always what you expect. Some harmful noises are clearly too loud and can even be painful to the ears of both children and adults. But the duration of exposure also plays a part in how damaging noises can be. Sometimes, a noise level or volume is harmless if heard briefly but can cause long-term hearing loss from prolonged exposure. An excellent example of this is a lawnmower. If a child walks by when someone is mowing their lawn, it’s unlikely to cause any harm. But if a child rides on a mower with a parent and hears the noise for an extended period without hearing protection, it can damage their hearing.

Navigating Your Child’s Development With Hearing Loss

After a diagnosis of hearing loss for your child, you may be wondering how you can be supportive and help your child navigate developmental stages.

To best support your child in growing and interacting with the world, you can follow the below tips.

Accept hearing loss. Hearing loss in children can be hard to accept for both the child and the parent(s). If your child has been diagnosed with hearing loss, you may be worried about their future. You may wonder how they’ll do in school if they struggle to fit in with peers or whether their hearing loss will affect future goals. Moving forward, accepting hearing loss will be part of your child’s life is an important step toward getting appropriate care. Acceptance isn’t easy, but it will encourage you and your child to manage their hearing health proactively.

Encourage goals and ambitions. Hearing loss doesn’t have to close doors. Children with hearing loss can have incredibly fulfilling lives — they can do well in school, have great friends, and even grow up to have thriving careers, social lives, and families. Encouraging a child’s dreams and goals reiterates that hearing loss does not mean they can’t achieve what they want.

Empower your child. Children like to feel capable and independent, which extends to their health. Encouraging a child to be active in hearing care is a great way to help them feel confident when caring for their ears, being responsible for their hearing devices, and addressing their hearing loss with others.

Engage family and friends. People often hesitate to discuss another person’s hearing loss, typically for fear of offending someone. Friends and family may avoid talking about your child’s hearing loss to ignore differences and make your child feel “normal.” Normalize hearing loss by encouraging discussion and being open to questions. Openly discussing their hearing challenges can help your child feel comfortable and not see it as embarrassing or different.

The general advice listed above is helpful for parents and children of all age groups, but there are also some age-specific considerations to keep in mind.

Infant and Toddler

During the first few years of life, exposure to various sounds, speech, and communication is essential for development. Make it a habit to talk to your child. Use different words, different sounds, different facial expressions, and even songs and music — exposure to variety will help a child with hearing loss connect with others and learn as much as they can about speech and communication.

Preschool

Preschool age is when children start to branch out personally and socially. They start having more (sometimes seemingly endless) questions about themselves, others, and the things around them. They begin testing their abilities intellectually and physically, helping them to gain confidence. They may be entering daycare or preschool and making friends for the first time.

It's important to encourage independence while establishing rules and enforcing structure during this stage. Children with hearing loss can begin to get involved in their hearing health at this age. They can be encouraged to ask questions, speak to their doctors directly, and be responsible for caring for hearing aids if they wear them.

School-age

Entering school brings new challenges for all children and unique challenges for children with hearing loss. They will be navigating new expectations for academic performance and focus while entering a social environment more complex than they’ve experienced before. Besides advocating for your child to get the support they need from school staff and administration, allowing and supporting your child's feelings is essential. They may be stressed, nervous, or overwhelmed, and it's helpful to accept these feelings while giving them time and privacy to work through them.

Teenagers

Teenagers begin to experience a new level of freedom and test their social interactions in many unique environments. As a teenager, your child will learn how to manage their time, foster friendships, maintain a social schedule, and develop new ways to make their hearing condition work with the world around them. Allow your teenager to define their role in the world while always being open to answering questions or helping. They’ll need to be independently functioning adults in a few short years.

Young Adults

When teens enter young adulthood, they may be on the verge of moving away from home, having serious relationships, or starting a career. They’ll want privacy, independence, and the ability to try to succeed on their own. Many parents of children with hearing loss find it difficult to back up and stop trying to help, but by now, your child has hopefully had access to resources and support that enable them to navigate the world with hearing loss.

Hearing Support for Children

Connecting a child with hearing loss to services and support as soon as it's suspected is crucial because early intervention is a critical factor in their development. Delayed assistance can lead to speech and communication delays, hindered academic progress, and emotional challenges. Prompt action maximizes their potential, fostering confidence and self-reliance, ultimately ensuring a brighter future for children with hearing loss.

Hearing Testing for Babies and Children

Whether you suspect hearing loss or not, having your child’s hearing tested is the best way to know if they need hearing support. There are different types of tests for babies and older children.

Hearing Tests for Babies

Otoacoustic Emissions (OAE) The OAE test measures whether parts of your baby’s ear respond to sound. A small earphone is placed in your baby’s ear canal to play sounds and measure an echo response from the baby's ear that should occur with normal hearing.

Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR) The ABR test measures how well your baby’s brain stem and auditory nerve carry sound from the ears to the brain. It's done by placing small, painless electrodes on your baby’s head and playing sounds through earphones.

Audiologists can perform both hearing tests while your baby is resting or sleeping. Newborns should have their hearing tested before leaving the hospital or as soon as possible after birth.

Hearing Tests for Children

Older children may have their hearing tested by OAE or ABR, but since children can typically give a verbal or other response to indicate whether they can hear certain sounds, there are other testing options.

Tympanometry An audiologist measures how well the eardrum moves by pushing air into the ear. Eardrum movement can determine if a child has wax buildup, excess fluid, an ear infection, or even a hole in the eardrum.

Acoustic Reflex Measures The acoustic reflex test checks the ear’s response to loud sounds. When an ear hears a loud sound, muscles in the middle ear contract, a process called acoustic reflex or middle ear muscle reflex (MEMR). Audiologists measure acoustic reflex through loud sounds sent through a rubber tip placed in the ear. A machine records the presence or lack of a reflex at each volume tested to detect hearing loss.

Pure-Tone Test In a pure-tone test, your child will wear headphones and listen to a series of tones played through them. They will indicate whether they hear a tone when it's played. The audiologist or other provider will vary the pitch and volume of the tones played to identify the quietest sounds the child can hear at various pitches.

Tuning Fork Test A tuning fork test uses a tuning fork to determine hearing loss in one or both ears. The provider will hold a tuning fork behind your child's ear or over their head and hit it to produce a tone. Your child will then indicate whether they hear the tone in one ear, both ears, or not at all.

Speech and Word Recognition An audiologist will ask your child to repeat words or phrases while wearing headphones. They may perform this test in various environments, like places with and without background noise. Speech and word recognition tests aim to determine the quietest volume your child can detect and process speech.

Hearing Aids and Devices

If your child has hearing loss, their audiologist may recommend using a device to assist their hearing. Popular options for children are hearing aids, cochlear implants, and FM systems.

Hearing Aids

Hearing aids are standard assistive devices for hearing loss, and some popular brands, like Oticon and Phonak, offer hearing aid models specifically designed for children. They adjust the tone, volume, and pitch of surrounding sounds to be better heard by people with hearing loss. Behind-the-ear models are preferred for babies and children, as they are easier to use and fit to small ears instead of smaller, more discreet designs.

Cochlear Implants

Cochlear implants use internal and external components to receive and process sound energy, allowing the brain to interpret it. Cochlear implants are for children with severe to profound hearing loss. Unlike hearing aids, which adjust the volume and tone of incoming sounds, cochlear implants bypass the cochlea entirely, so sounds are directed straight to the auditory nerve without being processed by the functional parts of the ear.

FM Systems

FM systems can benefit children trying to hear or focus in noisy environments like classrooms. They work with other hearing assistive devices, like hearing aids and cochlear implants. FM systems involve two parts — a microphone and transmitter worn by the speaker and a receiver used by the listener.

The receiver may be attached to the listener’s hearing aid or cochlear implant, an independent receiving device worn by the listener, or it may be a speaker located in the room or close to the listener. FM systems allow a listener with hearing loss to better and more directly focus on sounds they’re trying to hear.

Can Children Use Over-the-Counter Hearing Aids?

Over-the-counter hearing aids have increased hearing aid accessibility for many people, but OTC hearing aids are not appropriate for children. Hearing aids designed for children, or models that can be fit for children, are made to fit properly in small ears. OTC models follow more generic sizing to make them affordable and fit a wide variety of people.

Children must also get their hearing assessed and hearing aids properly fitted and tuned by an audiologist. Children may not know what sounds they’re not hearing or may not be able to express what they’re hearing, so professionals need to monitor how well their hearing aids are working for them.

Social Implications of Wearing Hearing Aids

Wearing hearing aids can be incredibly helpful for children with hearing loss but can also be a source of discomfort in social situations. Children (and sometimes people of all ages) may stare, ask questions, or pass judgment when they see someone with hearing aids. These situations are often impossible to avoid, but there are ways to prepare your child for when they find themselves involved in these interactions.

Prepare them for questions and practice responses. Preparation can help your child by eliminating the surprise they may feel when asked certain questions and giving them confident responses at the ready.

Connect your child with other hearing aid users. Getting involved with a community of people in similar situations will help your child to feel less different or alone and may help to ease feelings of being different.

Engage friends and classmates in conversations about your child's hearing. Being upfront about hearing loss and hearing aids keeps the topics from seeming shameful or embarrassing.

Empowering your child to educate others and speak openly about their hearing loss is important for building social confidence and helping them to navigate social settings.

Advocating for Your Child

Sending a child with hearing loss to school can be intimidating. Parents need to trust that educators are looking out for their children, but sometimes teachers need more information and guidance to give children the help they need to thrive. Communicate the basics of your child's hearing loss and individual needs to their teacher(s), and request a time to meet to discuss them in depth. Their teacher can then make decisions considering the child’s needs and ask important questions about accommodating them.

IEPs, or individualized education plans, define the services and accommodations the school system implements to meet the child’s needs. In public schools, there should be no out-of-pocket cost for any services necessary with your child’s IEP.

“IEPs are mandated in public schools but not private or charter schools,” says Rachel Magann Faivre, AuD, owner of Oklahoma City-based Ash Audiology. “So, there may be associated costs if a child doesn't attend a public school, and often the caregiver(s) have to play a more active role in ensuring their child receives the accommodations they need.” This means following up with teachers and administration to ensure they administer services as required.

Besides advocating for your child’s educational opportunities and accommodations, it's also important to be aware of your child’s social experiences. Students who seem “different” are more likely to be targeted by bullies than others. Children don’t always feel comfortable reporting bullying, but parents or guardians can report issues to the school and insist that incidents are investigated and dealt with appropriately.

Understanding hearing loss and advocating for a child's needs in school is essential for their social and academic success.
