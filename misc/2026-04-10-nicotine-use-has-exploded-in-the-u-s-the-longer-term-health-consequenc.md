# Nicotine Use Has Exploded in the U.S. The Longer-Term Health Consequences May Only Be Starting to Unfold.

This piece is part of a series of stories about nicotine's rebrand from a public health threat to a performance tool. Click here to read the rest of the stories.

EVAN BAEHR THOUGHT he had his body figured out. A clean-cut, 42-year-old tech entrepreneur and investor in Austin, Baehr lives the kind of performance-optimized, data-driven lifestyle that’s become so common in start-up culture. He works out five times a week. He takes 20 daily supplements, from B12 shots to creatine, plus TRT. He gets quarterly blood panels drawn at a boutique functional-medicine clinic that states “illness can become optional.” And recently, he started using nicotine pouches.

He first tried a pouch last spring, when another entrepreneur handed him one in his Cybertruck on the way to giving a talk out of town. “Try this—it’s great for energy,” the friend said. Baehr didn’t love the taste, but he was running on fumes after an exhausting day, and the little white pouch got him through his 7 o’clock presentation. He didn’t think much of it at the time. Within weeks, though, he was buying his own tins—a few bucks each for 15 pouches, available at groceries, gas stations, and convenience stores everywhere.

Surely you’ve seen nicotine pouches by now—most likely from the brand Zyn, the dominant player in a rapidly growing segment of the U.S. nicotine/tobacco industry (from about $4 billion in annual pouch sales in 2024 to an expected $19.4 billion by 2030, according to the market research firm Grand View Research). Small tea bag–like pouches of tobacco-free nicotine powder that you tuck between your gum and lip, they’re discreet and spit-free, and they don’t make you smell like smoke. And they deliver a stimulant buzz the podcasting Stanford professor Andrew Huberman has described as a “neurochemical attentional spotlight.”

While Huberman was careful to note the risks of nicotine in the episode he dedicated to the drug last year, other high-profile figures—Joe Rogan, Tucker Carlson, Baker Mayfield, Josh Allen, Josh Brolin, and even Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who was spotted slipping in a pouch during his Senate confirmation hearing—have offered tacit endorsements simply by using pouches.

To Baehr, who did not previously use nicotine, the pouches felt like magic: no jitters, no crash, a quiet hum of concentration that made 16-hour days feel doable—if he just kept slipping in new pouches every few hours. In less than six months he was up to 35 milligrams per day, or roughly the equivalent nicotine intake of smoking a pack of cigarettes daily. And then his latest lab results came back.

His A1C, an indicator of blood-sugar control that’s part of his quarterly blood panel, had jumped from 5.1 to 5.6, edging into prediabetic range. Nothing about the rest of his health screams prediabetic: He has a VO2 max in the 50s and a heart rate variability in the 80s—extraordinary numbers for a 42-year-old office jockey. “Basically, I’m a superathlete,” he told me. “And adding nicotine made me a diabetic.”

Well, maybe. It’s tough to say conclusively that nicotine was responsible for the lightning-fast change in his case, but researchers have indeed demonstrated a connection between nicotine and insulin resistance, which can lead to elevated blood sugar levels. (Ironically, quitting can cause the same effect, as the body adjusts to life without nicotine.)

The drug’s risks don’t end there. Nicotine constricts blood vessels, raises heart rate and blood pressure, and puts extra strain on the cardiovascular system, says Tory Spindle, PhD, an associate professor at Johns Hopkins who studies the behavioral pharmacology of nicotine and tobacco. Prolonged use of pouches can irritate the mouth and gums and cause precancerous lesions (though there’s not a demonstrated link between pouch use and oral cancer). Above all, nicotine remains profoundly addictive—about as much so as cocaine and heroin. Still, Spindle says, “certainly compared to smoking, nicotine pouches are far safer, with far fewer carcinogens.”

Therein lies the paradox of modern nicotine. A substance that until very recently was inextricably linked to smoking and dipping—and lung and oral cancer and other often fatal conditions, such as the lung diseases COPD and emphysema—has, in the space of a few years, gone from vice to virtue, or at least something close. As a former smoker myself and the parent of a kid who’s approaching the age when I first got hooked, I can’t help but despair at the idea of this drug acquiring a halo of cool once again. The last time I saw nicotine look this seductive, the Marlboro Man was selling freedom on horseback. Now it’s in TikToks that make life on Zyn look like the secret handshake of ambition (the hashtag #zyn logged more than 400 million views in a single month in 2024).

It’s a dizzying rebrand not just because of the 180-degree reputation change but also because of how quickly the market has exploded. The business of nicotine today looks like a kind of Wild West gold rush, where everyone from wellness types to profiteering speculators to Big Tobacco itself is trying to get in before researchers and regulators can figure out what’s going on—let alone what to do about it. Standing in the balance is a whole new generation at risk of finding itself in the grip of an industry with more blood on its hands than almost any other in modern history—an industry whose profit margins have historically rivaled Big Oil’s and whose model has always depended on keeping people coming back, whatever form the product takes.

A Gray Area

If there’s a single moment that sums up the cluster of contradictions defining the new nicotine boom, it came last January, when the FDA officially authorized 20 Zyn products for sale—the first nicotine pouches ever to win a green light from the federal government to be sold in the U.S. What should have been a clarifying move by the regulator instead was something like a record scratch—wait, what?—and prompted more questions than answers.

For one thing, if Zyn was only now officially authorized, after years on the market, how does the FDA continue to allow dozens of other brands to sell pouches without full authorization? Perhaps more fundamentally, why would the FDA allow the sale of a substance that had for decades been at the center of a public health crisis?

To understand the roots of the nicotine rebrand, rewind to 2016. That year, the FDA’s so-called deeming rule extended the agency’s authority to e-cigarettes, otherwise known as vapes—effectively legitimizing and regulating them instead of banning them outright. The move didn’t just bring a booming gray-market industry under federal oversight; it also, in practice, separated nicotine from tobacco. For the first time, there was a sanctioned way to use the drug that didn’t involve lighting a cigarette.

In anticipation of the 2016 deeming rule taking effect, tobacco companies began a flurry of launching, acquiring, and investing in e-cigarette brands, positioning themselves as part of the solution to a public-health crisis rather than the problem. Thanks to its sleek, small device that looks like a computer flash drive, e-cigarette brand Juul—with substantial investment from Marlboro parent Altria—would ignite a full-blown youth craze built on the same premise: nicotine without smoke (among high schoolers, use jumped from 1.5 percent in 2011 to nearly 21 percent in 2018). What older generations associated with smoke and disease now arrived as modern, flavored, and almost innocuous. Meanwhile, in Sweden, another evolution was taking shape, as a company called Swedish Match, a maker of popular tobacco pouches called snus, quietly launched a fully tobacco-free pouch that delivered nicotine discreetly without even the stigma of a vapor cloud. Its name was Zyn.

So when the FDA officially authorized the sale of Zyn products (while imposing “stringent marketing restrictions” on how they could be promoted in digital media and on radio and television), it wasn’t the start of a radical new chapter so much as the continuation of one. As a tobacco product regulated by the FDA’s Center for Tobacco Products, a pouch “doesn’t need to prove that it’s a drug that is safe and effective,” explains epidemiologist Brittney Keller-Hamilton, PhD, an associate professor at Ohio State University who studies nicotine products. “It just needs to prove that it’s what the FDA calls ‘appropriate for the protection of public health,’ ” a legal standard that simply means it’s a better alternative to cigarettes.

It sounds straightforward—these products are either authorized or not—but the messy reality is that many products hit shelves long before they’ve been reviewed. And because the FDA has been deluged with applications, it has allowed companies to continue selling while the review process plays out. “The speed with which the FDA is reviewing and authorizing products for market is suboptimal,” says Cristine Delnevo, PhD, director of the Rutgers Institute for Nicotine & Tobacco Studies. The result is a regulatory gray zone where rules exist but enforcement is patchy at best. Some brands on the market haven’t filed for authorization at all.

In September, the FDA tried to bring some order to the chaos, announcing a pilot program to fast-track authorization for several more brands beyond Zyn, with a goal of completing reviews by the end of the year. Notably, it specified that it would prioritize reviewing products made by the tobacco giants Philip Morris International (which now owns Zyn), Altria Group, Reynolds American, and Turning Point Brands—a move those companies have encouraged.

The FDA and the tobacco companies, in fact, are surprisingly aligned in how they talk about pouches and the future of nicotine. When the agency framed its fast-track process as a way to accelerate access to “non-combusted tobacco products that can generally pose lower health risks,” it echoed how Philip Morris International talks about a “smoke-free future” and Altria talks about “moving beyond smoking.”

The motives may differ—the agency needs to regulate, the companies need to hang on to a customer base that’s literally addicted to giving them money—but the result is a shared strategy known as harm reduction. For regulators, harm reduction is good because it saves lives. For tobacco companies, harm reduction is good because it saves the business. Long used in public health circles to generate strategies like needle exchanges and seat-belt laws, the harm-reduction philosophy is essentially a “don’t let perfect be the enemy of good” approach to managing threats. It came into play with nicotine when the FDA gained authority over tobacco products in 2009. “While cessation is always the best outcome,” explains Delnevo, “reduced-risk products can still be important if they help people move away from smoking.”

Sweden offers a glimpse of what that looks like in the long run. National policy changes around tobacco in that country dramatically decreased cigarette smoking and paved the way for an increase in nicotine pouch use, a shift credited with driving down rates of cancer and heart disease. The irony, of course, is that Sweden’s public-health success also kicked off a gold rush in this country, where a generation of entrepreneurs saw an opportunity to repackage an old addiction as innovation—each with its own spin on how to sell it.

Decades of pharmacology research—going back to the 1980s—had established thatnicotine could provide a cognitive boost, but it was only after Zyn’s marketing combated the substance’s negative associations that the productivity-hacker and wellness elite began to talk about it as a tool. Juul had already done the heavy lifting of divorcing nicotine from combustion and spitting; Zyn simply took that baton and removed even Juul’s remaining stigma—no clouds, no visible device that mimicked a cigarette. By the early 2020s, pouch sales were booming in this country, and the same dopamine-driven effects that once made cigarettes addictive were now being reframed as a cognitive edge.

Much as the FDA’s categorization of pouches as tobacco products prevents brands from selling them as smoking-cessation aids like Nicorette, by extension they also can’t make functional health claims like cognitive or physical performance enhancement. And yet there are companies like Athletic Nicotine, for instance, whose website features a fit woman on a rowing machine with the text “Taste Victory. Next-Level Nicotine for the Winner in All of Us.” Beneath it, big-wave surfer Luca Padua endorses the product: “Many of the best tools are the most simple, and this is one of them. Athletic Nicotine is an instrument for performance.” Athletic Nicotine declined to comment on the claims.

A start-up called Shift, meanwhile, tests the limits of talking about itself as a smoking-cessation tool. Rather than selling its pouches directly, Shift charges for a “low-nicotine plan” that offers “step-by-step guidance on how to taper effectively.” The nicotine pouches themselves are “included at no additional cost to support the tapering process,” as the Shift site puts it—a framing that positions them as part of an educational service rather than a stand-alone product. “Shift does not sell nicotine replacement therapy products and makes no claims about helping users quit smoking,” the company tells Men’s Health. “The goal is to give adults structure, transparency, and data as they adjust their own consumption.”

More pernicious, researchers say, is the way nicotine pouches are being flavored and sold. In the 2024 National Youth Tobacco Survey, more than 8 in 10 young people who use nicotine pouches reported using flavored ones, most often mint, followed by fruit. By law, you must be 21 or older to buy pouches, but generations of kids have gotten around age restrictions for cigarettes, dip, and vapes, and it’s easier than ever today with online sales. Several states have banned flavored tobacco products altogether (which includes pouches in D.C. and California), yet brands built around flavor remain among the fastest-growing.

The marketing playbook looks familiar: Candy-colored tins and bright flavors such as mango and watermelon strawberry mint echo the sweet, approachable branding that helped fuel the youth vaping boom a decade ago—only this time the product comes with a health halo. “Anytime we see flavors being marketed with [nicotine], we’re concerned that young people, kids, are going to start using the product,” says Meghan Moran, PhD, a behavioral scientist at Johns Hopkins who studies how marketing and media shape tobacco use. “It’s a main way to make the product more palatable and easy to use, so that they may continue using it for the taste and then become addicted.”

Spindle, the Johns Hopkins professor, takes that argument a step further. “I worry that it’s normalizing nicotine,” he says. “We’ve made a lot of progress in bringing down smoking rates among youth, but pouches could undo some of that. Maybe young people start using it for the same reasons adults do—for cognitive enhancement, for performance—and that results in addiction and then going on to more harmful products. That’s what we saw early on with e-cigarettes—they were leading to people smoking cigarettes.” It’s a dark irony that a product described as a safer off-ramp from cigarettes could end up steering a new generation toward them.

The science backs up Spindle’s concern. Studies show that nicotine dependence can develop in adolescents within weeks of first exposure—sometimes after only a few uses—and that teenage brains, still wiring their reward systems, are particularly susceptible. Once that circuitry is shaped by nicotine, the cravings can last for years, if not a lifetime.

I know about that all too well. I was in middle school when I first tried a cigarette, and I couldn’t stop thinking about it that night—about the rush it gave me, about how cool and grown-up I thought it made me. It’s one of my most vivid memories of adolescence, and decades later I can still conjure the feeling.

Truth and Consequences

In my case, the rush of nicotine led to years of inhaling smoke—willfully doing something that kills nearly half a million people every year in the U.S. alone. “Cigarettes are the most dangerous form of tobacco product and kill one of every two users if used as intended,” says Delnevo at Rutgers. Combustion—the burning of tobacco—creates tar, carbon monoxide, formaldehyde, and thousands of other toxins that ignite inflammation, damage blood vessels, and disrupt the DNA-repair systems that keep cells from turning cancerous.

Nicotine without the smoke is safer by every toxicological measure, yet it’s still capable of ensnaring the brain as powerfully as ever. A stimulant that binds to neural receptors in the brain and body, nicotine triggers the release of dopamine, norepinephrine, acetylcholine, and serotonin—the same chemical messengers that regulate focus, arousal, and mood. In small doses, that mix can sharpen attention and steady mood, creating the calm-alert feeling many pouch users describe.

Nicotine is also what makes it hard to quit—whether you’re smoking, vaping, dipping, or using pouches. Addiction develops through both positive reinforcement (it feels good, so you use again) and negative reinforcement (you use to avoid withdrawal), Spindle explains. “Most people can relate to the analogy of coffee,” he says. That first cup in the morning offers a nice jolt that you want to replicate. “But if you missed your morning cup, you start to feel crappy, you have a headache, maybe you just feel a little bit tired. And then if you drink your cup of coffee, you snap out of it and you feel back to normal eventually. That’s the same thing that happens with people who use nicotine long enough. They start to have to use nicotine to basically feel normal and feel like they’re at their baseline.”

That cycle isn’t just psychological. Each hit of nicotine floods the brain’s receptor sites, overstimulating them until the neurons adapt by making more of those receptors and reducing their sensitivity—a process known as upregulation. Over time, your brain’s chemistry recalibrates around the constant presence of nicotine. The drug becomes the new normal; without it, dopamine levels drop, leaving you foggy and irritable until the next dose restores equilibrium.

Among heavy pouch users, that maintenance loop can run all day. I remember that loop well from my nicotine days—how automatic it became to reach for a cigarette, how lighting up stopped feeling less like a decision and more like a reflex. It’s a story I heard again and again as I talked to heavy users of pouches. One user I spoke with, an entrepreneur friend of Evan Baehr’s, says he uses nicotine pouches “from the moment I wake up until the moment I fall asleep—and I know a lot of people who sleep with them.”

And dosage size creeps up over time. Three milligrams is the smallest dose Zyn offers, and six milligrams is the best selling, according to Keller-Hamilton. Some pouch brands go up to 12 mg or higher, but legally there’s no limit, and some European brands available online can reach as high as 50 mg. “The threes wear off and you need to up it to a six,” says Evan Loomis, the friend who first introduced Baehr to pouches. When we spoke, he was raising money for his investment fund and popping Zyns before meetings “because you have to bring energy through the phone or Zoom. People need to get the feeling that you’re going to run through every brick wall to make this thing successful.”

Loomis was hoping to ease back down to lower doses after completing the fundraising, but he knows other high-performance types, including one of his friends, who have only continued to ramp up. “He’ll pop in two eights at a time,” Loomis says, “and he does that 10 times a day.” That’s 160 milligrams of nicotine per day, the equivalent of as many as four packs of smokes.

That’s where the habit potentially gets more dangerous. In studies, a single cigarette raises heart rate by roughly 10 to 15 beats per minute and systolic blood pressure by 5 to 10 points. Pouches produce milder bumps than cigarettes, but they scale with dose. “Generally speaking, at a higher dose you’d expect increases in all the physiological and cognitive measures—heart rate, blood pressure, perceived stimulation,” says Spindle.

Keller-Hamilton is concerned about the social media trend of young people taking extreme amounts of nicotine. “We see ‘Zyn challenges’ on TikTok, where people keep adding more and more pouches to their mouth,” she says, “which could lead to nicotine overdose or poisoning.”

Moreover, she adds, if people use large amounts of nicotine for long periods of time—those who regularly double up on pouches, for instance, a practice known as double-decking—their baseline heart rate can start creeping up. “Because they’re always exposed to this nicotine, it’s adding stress to their cardiovascular system.” For now, it’s not clear to anyone how widespread that kind of use is, and Keller-Hamilton hopes to study these users in the future “to see how their bodies are adjusting and if we’re veering into really unsafe cardiovascular territory.”

The truth is, there’s still a lot we don’t know about who’s using nicotine pouches and how much. The best national data gives only a blurry outline. As Keller-Hamilton points out, although sales are soaring, actual use remains limited to a specific band of the population—mostly men, mostly white, and mostly people who already smoked or used other nicotine products. Among kids, use is small but climbing. In 2024, 1.8 percent of middle and high school students—roughly 480,000 of them—said they’d used nicotine pouches, up from 1.1 percent in 2022, a 64 percent jump in just two years.

But those numbers come from sweeping national surveys that can’t yet capture what’s happening in smaller, faster-moving corners of the culture. “Anecdotally, we hear that there are pockets of people in certain sectors who are starting to use nicotine pouches when they weren’t already using other nicotine sources,” Keller-Hamilton says. “The national surveys haven’t drilled down to those populations yet.” She’s also seen anecdotal increases on college campuses.

Moran sees evidence of higher-use pockets in performance-driven settings: athletes chasing focus, firefighters staying sharp on shift, soldiers looking for a steadier buzz than dip or cigarettes offer. One recent JAMA study found that 24 percent of active-duty service members had used nicotine pouches in the past 30 days—many times higher than the national average.

And then there’s the tribe Baehr belongs to, what he calls “type-A CEO people.” His own clinician estimated that as many as 60 percent of her high-performing clients are on nicotine in one form or another. For now, those numbers live mostly in anecdotes and subcultures, waiting for the data to catch up.

The Future

My son is in middle school. He knows what vaping is—kids vape in the bathrooms between classes, so much so that vape detectors are being installed in schools throughout the country—but when I asked the other day if he’d ever heard of nicotine pouches, he looked at me like I was speaking in tongues. He hadn’t. Not yet.

It was a reminder of how quickly these things can change. A decade ago, vaping was barely a curiosity—a cleaner way to smoke—and then, almost overnight, it was everywhere. Part of the reason, as Rutgers researcher Cristine Delnevo points out, is that regulation arrived far too late. Although the FDA formally gained authority over e-cigarettes in 2016, real oversight didn’t begin until 2020, due to delays with the FDA’s premarket review process—by which time Juul and a wave of youth-oriented vapes had already flooded in. Delnevo’s trying to prevent a sequel. “The FDA must act swiftly to exercise its regulatory authority,” she wrote in a JAMA editorial a year ago, “both by granting marketing orders for nicotine pouch products that meet its standards and penalizing those that violate the law, not allowing this newest segment of the market to evolve as uncontrollably as e-cigarettes.”

So far, aside from the FDA’s move to authorize a few of the major brands’ products, there’s little sign the Trump administration is listening. Last April, the administration slashed federal funding to the CDC’s Office on Smoking and Health, which plays a critical role in preventing youth tobacco use and helping adult tobacco users quit. This led to the cancellation of the 2025 National Youth Tobacco Survey, whose results were highly anticipated to see if anecdotal evidence of increased nicotine use would show up in the official data. As of press time, the study’s fate is still unknown. Meanwhile, the Make Our Children Healthy Again strategy report, written by the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) Commission—a sweeping blueprint released by the White House for tackling chronic disease in children—includes a brief call to crack down on illegal vaping products but otherwise says nothing about nicotine or tobacco use.

That silence can be read two ways. One is as confidence that the fight is largely won. Smoking, vaping, and most other nicotine uses are trending down, and even though their popularity is growing, pouches seem far less harmful. The other is as a blind spot. A new generation is finding its way to a highly addictive drug through a cleaner, subtler form, and the long-term story may only be beginning.

After his blood-work scare, Baehr cut back on his nicotine usage, from a can a day to a few 3-milligram pouches. But he hasn’t quit. He likes the buzz, and he’s finding it useful. He’s currently in the early stages of building an AI company—“and we call it Zyn-powered,” he says. He wears a continuous glucose monitor now, watching how his body responds as he experiments with dosage and timing. Like so many things in the performance-optimization world, he has turned his pouch habit into a data problem.

That’s the seduction of the nicotine rebrand: a stimulant promising control and productivity. Until you realize who’s actually in charge.
