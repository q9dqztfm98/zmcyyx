Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Doctors typically diagnose stomach ulcers by first reviewing your medical history, including NSAID use, H. pylori infection, and lifestyle factors. They then assess the onset, timing, and meal-related pattern of your symptoms before performing a focused abdominal exam to check for tenderness, guarding, or signs of internal bleeding.

Common warning signs of a stomach ulcer include:

Burning upper-abdominal pain

Bloating and heartburn

Nausea

Unintended weight loss

Black stools or coffee-ground vomit (signs of bleeding)

Explanation

A peptic ulcer—also known as a stomach or duodenal ulcer—is a sore that forms in the lining of your stomach or the first part of your small intestine. Millions of people worldwide develop peptic ulcers each year. Recognizing early warning signs and knowing what doctors focus on can speed diagnosis, guide treatment and help prevent complications.

What Is a Peptic Ulcer?

A break in the protective inner lining of the stomach (gastric ulcer) or duodenum (duodenal ulcer).

Most ulcers are linked to infection by Helicobacter pylori bacteria or long-term use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen.

Other risk factors include smoking, excessive alcohol use, stress and certain medical conditions.

Peptic ulcers can be painful and, if untreated, may lead to bleeding or perforation. Early evaluation helps manage symptoms and reduces the chance of serious issues.

Common Symptoms of a Peptic Ulcer

People with peptic ulcers often describe a variety of digestive complaints. Although symptoms can overlap with other conditions, doctors look for characteristic patterns:

Burning or gnawing pain in the upper abdomen• Often occurs 1–3 hours after eating or during the night• May briefly improve after eating (duodenal ulcer) or worsen (gastric ulcer)

Bloating and fullness

Heartburn or acid reflux

Nausea or vomiting

Loss of appetite and unintended weight loss

Belching or burping

Black or tarry stools (a sign of bleeding)

Vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds

Remember that not everyone experiences all these signs. Some people—especially older adults—may have minimal or atypical symptoms.

Why Early Detection Matters

Untreated peptic ulcers can lead to:

Internal bleeding, causing anemia or life-threatening blood loss

Perforation (a hole through the stomach wall), risking severe infection

Gastric outlet obstruction, where swelling blocks passage of food

What Doctors Ask and Look for First

When you visit a healthcare provider with possible ulcer symptoms, they typically:

Review Your Medical History

Use of NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen)

History of H. pylori infection or treatment

Family history of ulcers or gastrointestinal cancers

Smoking and alcohol use

Stressors or other medical conditions (e.g., liver disease, inflammatory bowel disease)

Discuss Symptom Details

Onset, duration and pattern of the pain

Relationship to meals or time of day

Any factors that relieve or worsen symptoms

Presence of alarming signs (bleeding, weight loss)

Perform a Physical Examination

Gentle abdominal palpation to check for tenderness, guarding or rigidity

Listening for bowel sounds

Checking for signs of anemia (pale skin, rapid heartbeat)

Examining stool or vomit if there's concern about bleeding

Diagnostic Tests and Procedures

To confirm a peptic ulcer and identify its cause, doctors may recommend:

Upper Endoscopy (EGD)

A thin, flexible tube with a camera is passed through the mouth into the stomach and duodenum.

Allows direct visualization and biopsy to check for H. pylori or cancer cells.

During an endoscopic exam, your doctor evaluates:

Size, number and location of ulcers

Presence of bleeding: active oozing or healed scar tissue

Inflammation of the stomach lining (gastritis)

Biopsy results to rule out cancer and detect H. pylori

This information guides both the choice and duration of treatment.

Initial Management and Treatment

[Symptoms What Doctors](https://insulin-pathways.hbsnzj.com/)

Once diagnosed, treatment focuses on relieving pain, healing the ulcer and preventing recurrence:

Eradication of H. pylori (if present)• Combination of two antibiotics plus a proton-pump inhibitor (PPI) for 10–14 days

Acid suppression• PPIs (omeprazole, lansoprazole) or H2 blockers (ranitidine) to reduce acidity

Stopping or reducing NSAID use• Switching to alternative pain relief (acetaminophen)• Adding protective medications if NSAIDs are essential

Lifestyle modifications• Avoid smoking and limit alcohol• Eat smaller, more frequent meals• Manage stress through relaxation techniques

Most ulcers begin to heal within weeks of starting therapy. Your doctor will schedule follow-up to confirm healing, especially if you had bleeding or a complicated ulcer.

Warning Signs: When to Seek Help Immediately

Some ulcer complications can be life threatening. Seek emergency care if you experience:

Sudden, severe abdominal pain

Vomiting large amounts of blood (bright red or coffee-ground appearance)

Black, tarry stools or bright red blood in stools

Dizziness, fainting or a rapid heartbeat (possible shock)

Unexplained, rapid weight loss and ongoing vomiting

Early intervention can be lifesaving in these situations.

Peptic ulcers can often be managed effectively when caught early. Doctors focus on your history, physical exam and targeted tests to confirm the diagnosis and rule out more serious problems.

• Keep track of your symptoms and note any patterns.• Avoid self-medicating with high-dose NSAIDs for extended periods.• Complete the full course of prescribed antibiotics and acid-reducing medications.• Follow up as directed to ensure healing and reduce the risk of recurrence.

Always remember: if you suspect bleeding, severe pain or any potentially life-threatening complication, speak to a doctor or go to the nearest emergency department right away. Your health and safety come first.
