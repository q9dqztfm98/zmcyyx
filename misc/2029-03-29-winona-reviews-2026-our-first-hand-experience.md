Effectiveness

Winona’s primary offering is hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for menopause symptoms, which is typically either a prescription of just estrogen or a combination of estrogen and progesterone.5 Winona’s available HRT treatments include both estrogen and combination therapy in the form of pills, creams, and patches.

Whether from Winona or elsewhere, HRT has quite a bit of potential to help women experiencing a wide range of menopause symptoms, including but not limited to:4 5

Hot flashes

Night sweats

Painful intercourse

Vaginal dryness

Vaginal itching

Estrogen and progesterone play significant roles in your body, and as their production changes during menopause, “supplementing” them with HRT can benefit your health in several ways outside of menopause symptom relief. For example, estrogen can help lower your cholesterol and promote glucose metabolism.6

Two of Winona’s other treatments — DHEA and Sildenafil Arousal Cream — have mixed or limited supporting evidence at this time.3 DHEA may help improve vaginal atrophy, while research on topical sildenafil suggests it may improve arousal in some women, particularly those with arousal-specific concerns, though results haven’t been consistent across larger studies.7 8 41

Safety

For most healthy adults, HRT should be a relatively safe and effective treatment option for menopause symptoms when prescribed appropriately.5 9 However, Winona’s approach deserves additional scrutiny because the company primarily uses compounded bioidentical hormone replacement therapy (HRT), an area that remains somewhat controversial within menopause care.

Unlike standard FDA-approved HRT products, compounded medications are customized formulations prepared by a compounding pharmacy.14 These treatments are not subjected to the same manufacturing, consistency, and purity standards as FDA-approved medications.

To its credit, Winona’s compounding pharmacy operates under Idaho Board of Pharmacy regulations requiring equipment to be cleaned, sanitized, or sterilized before use, and active ingredients must be sourced from FDA-registered manufacturers.15 Winona also states that its compounded treatments are made using FDA-approved ingredients, though the resulting customized medications are not FDA-approved themselves.16

Beyond the additional considerations surrounding compounded medications, HRT itself is not appropriate for everyone. Certain medical conditions and risk factors may increase the likelihood of complications, including:5 39 40

Breast cancer

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE)

Gallbladder disease

Liver disease

Coronary artery disease or heart disease

Stroke

Dementia (including those with a higher risk for dementia)

Certain cancers

However, many experts believe the benefits of HRT can outweigh these risks for otherwise healthy people seeking relief from moderate to severe menopause symptoms.5 9

We should also note that Winona strongly markets its treatments as “bioidentical” or “natural,” meaning the hormones are chemically identical to those naturally produced by the human body.10 While this may sound inherently safer or more advanced, current evidence does not clearly show that compounded bioidentical HRT is safer than traditional FDA-approved hormone therapy.11 In fact, some FDA-approved HRT medications are also bioidentical and use similar or identical ingredients.10 12 13

Overall, Winona’s safety profile appears reasonably comparable to many other HRT providers, but its reliance on compounded hormone formulations differs from competitors like Alloy and Gennev, which primarily use FDA-approved medications instead.

Gennev could also be a strong option if your insurance is accepted.

Evernow’s pricing is more variable. In addition to a membership fee (typically about $35–$49 per month, depending on the plan), medications are priced separately. Tablets cost around $27.50 per month, while patches range from about $59.99 to $89.99 for a 30-day supply.

This means Evernow can be competitive with — or even less expensive than — Winona in some cases, particularly for tablet-based treatments. However, total costs depend on how you access care. With Evernow, using insurance may reduce medication costs if prescriptions are filled through a local pharmacy, while medications shipped directly to your home are typically cash-pay. Evernow’s membership fees aren’t covered by insurance, though the company’s one-time $150 visit may be eligible depending on your plan.

For those with an HSA/FSA plan, Winona can accept it at checkout or provide documentation for reimbursement. Alloy and Gennev offer similar support, while Evernow allows HSA/FSA use for membership costs, but not for medications unless they’re filled through a local pharmacy.

What is compounding?

The majority of Winona’s prescriptions are compounded, which means they’re customized to fit the needs of an individual patient. Generally speaking, compounding can take many forms, including but not limited to:16 19

Modifying the delivery method (e.g., making a liquid for someone who can’t take pills)

Offering a dose strength that isn’t commercially available

Providing allergen-free forms of a drug (e.g., when someone is allergic to a dye present in the FDA-approved version of a medication)

Combining two or more drugs into one

Filling the gap when a drug shortage occurs

In Winona’s case, compounding entails combining drugs and providing personalized doses of its available prescription treatments. Unlike your local compounding pharmacy, Winona won’t change how a medication is taken (such as turning a pill into a liquid), remove allergens (like the peanut oil in its progesterone capsules), or create brand-new combinations outside of what it already offers, but it will try to customize doses to fit your needs.

When it comes to compounding drugs, there are two possible preparation methods: sterile and nonsterile. Winona’s pharmacy uses the latter.19 The term “nonsterile” may raise a few concerned eyebrows, but this type of preparation still requires a sanitary environment. Capsules, tablets, creams, solutions, and similar medications are generally compounded using nonsterile preparation, whereas sterile compounding is typically reserved for implantable, injectable, and ophthalmic (eye) drugs.19

Insider Tip: It’s important to mention that compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. Even if they’re made entirely of FDA-approved ingredients (like Winona’s treatments), the combination itself is not approved. That doesn’t mean the treatments are inherently unsafe, but that using them may come with additional risks due to their lack of FDA verification.16

Do Winona’s treatments work?

Whether Winona’s treatments are effective largely depends on how well HRT works overall. Most of the company’s offerings are hormone-based, with one exception: Sildenafil Arousal Cream, a topical formula containing sildenafil (commonly used in Viagra) and pentoxifylline, a vasodilator.

Even though Winona promotes its compounded HRT as “bioidentical,” there’s no clear evidence that this distinction improves efficacy over traditional HRT treatments.11 Several FDA-approved HRT medications are also bioidentical and use the same ingredients.10

Because they’re extremely similar, information on the effectiveness of FDA-approved HRT medications can also be applied to Winona’s treatments.

HRT

If HRT is a suitable treatment for you, it can relieve symptoms like vaginal dryness or itching, hot flashes, and night sweats.5 It can also reduce your chance of developing osteoporosis. One study found that hormone therapy reduced the overall risk of bone fractures, regardless of fall risk.28

Additionally, systemic HRT may improve libido and sexual responsiveness in menopausal women.38

DHEA

Research is limited and mixed, but DHEA may be able to help treat or prevent osteoporosis and improve vaginal atrophy in postmenopausal women.7 25

Sildenafil Arousal Cream (sildenafil and pentoxifylline)

Research on topical sildenafil and pentoxifylline for female sexual arousal is still fairly limited. Sildenafil is commonly used in Viagra, while pentoxifylline is a vasodilator that may help increase blood flow when applied to the skin.29 One early study found that sildenafil cream increased “genital and self-reported measures of arousal compared to placebo cream” in a group of six women, which is promising — but obviously a very small sample.8 More recent research on topical sildenafil suggests it may help some women, particularly those with arousal-specific concerns, though it didn’t show clear benefits across the full study population.41 The good news is that sildenafil cream appears to be well tolerated in clinical trials, with few reported side effects.17

This type of HRT — estrogen therapy (ET) — is only prescribed to women without a uterus (those who’ve had a hysterectomy) since it can increase the risk of uterine cancer.5 26 When used in this population, ET has fewer long-term risks than combination therapy. In fact, ET may significantly reduce the risk of developing breast cancer, though more research and trials are necessary before confirming this possibility.34

Combination estrogen and progesterone

Combined hormone therapy (sometimes called estrogen-progestin therapy, or EPT) almost acts like ET’s opposite when it comes to cancer risk. Combination therapy reduces the risk of uterine cancer due to the inclusion of progesterone, but this addition may increase the chance of developing breast cancer.5 34 Combination therapy may also slightly increase the risk of ovarian cancer.35

DHEA is a hormone naturally produced by the body, though supplemental forms are typically synthesized in a lab. In the United States, it’s available over the counter and does not require a prescription. Strangely enough, Winona’s patient dashboard summarizes its potential side effects as “Non-rx should not have.” However, this characterization is misleading, as DHEA is associated with a range of potential side effects and drug interactions.

DHEA may increase the risk of mania in people with mood disorders, and it can interact with a wide range of prescription drugs, including those for mental health, like SSRIs and antipsychotics. DHEA might also increase the risk of hormone-sensitive cancers, and it can even interact with prescription estrogens, leading to nausea, headaches, and insomnia.

If you’re nursing or pregnant, you should avoid DHEA. And according to the Mayo Clinic, it may be best to avoid this supplement entirely due to the safety risks.7 25

Capsules (progesterone and DHEA)

While the capsule isn’t an active ingredient, it’s important to point out that Winona’s capsules all contain gelatin, and the progesterone ones contain peanut oil. That means none of Winona’s capsules are vegan-friendly, and the progesterone capsules should be avoided by people with peanut allergies.

Sildenafil

Research on topical sildenafil is still fairly limited, but newer clinical trials suggest it’s generally well tolerated, with few systemic side effects reported.17 That said, most long-term safety data still comes from studies on oral sildenafil, which may cause headaches, nausea, flushing, nasal congestion, and other mild side effects.

More serious reactions — such as vision changes, chest pain, fainting, and allergic responses — are rare but have been reported with oral use.36 While these risks may be less likely with topical application, they can’t be ruled out entirely due to limited long-term data.

Pentoxifylline

Perhaps the most significant risk of using pentoxifylline is the fact that it’s an FDA Pregnancy Category C ingredient. Category C ingredients “have shown an adverse effect on the fetus” in animal studies. Pentoxifylline should be avoided by those who are pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning to become pregnant — meaning that Sildenafil Arousal Cream should be avoided, too.37

Much of the same safety information applies to the prescriptions available through Winona’s close competitors, like Gennev, Evernow, and Alloy. While they may offer different forms of HRT delivery (Alloy, for instance, has an estradiol spray option), the active ingredients remain the same.

Winona’s pharmacy

Based on our review of both state and federal regulations, along with information provided directly by the company, Winona’s compounding pharmacies appear to follow a strong set of safety standards.

A key distinction is that compounded medications are not reviewed or approved by the FDA.16 Because of this, it’s especially important to understand the regulatory framework these pharmacies operate under, whether you’re using Winona or another provider.

Winona states that it owns and operates two 503A compounding pharmacies, which create prescriptions on a patient-specific basis rather than mass-producing medications.31 32 These types of pharmacies are primarily regulated at the state level, though they must still comply with applicable portions of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act).31

According to Winona, its pharmacies are subject to oversight from state Boards of Pharmacy and must follow United States Pharmacopeia (USP) compounding standards for quality, purity, strength, and identity. The company also notes that all compounded prescriptions are made using FDA-approved active ingredients, even though the final formulations themselves are not FDA-approved.

More specifically, Winona’s compounding pharmacy is located in Idaho, where the Board of Pharmacy outlines preparation requirements under “Idaho Admin. Code r. 24.36.01.700.” These include:15

All equipment must be cleaned, sanitized, or sterilized prior to use.

All active ingredients must be obtained from FDA-registered manufacturers.

Weighing and measuring must be accurate; all ingredient amounts “must not vary” from the acceptable range listed in the United States Pharmacopeia and National Formulary (USP-NF), a set of quality standards for medications and ingredients.

A certificate of analysis (COA) must be obtained for all active pharmaceutical ingredients used in compounding.

Overall, while compounded medications carry additional considerations compared to FDA-approved drugs, the available regulatory information suggests that Winona’s pharmacy operates within established safety guidelines.

Alternatives to Winona

If you aren’t entirely sold on Winona, or you want to learn more about other telehealth HRT treatment options, here are some details on a few of Winona’s close competitors.

Gennev

The biggest difference between Gennev and Winona is that Gennev accepts certain insurance plans — currently, Aetna, Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield, and Cigna Healthcare plans. The coverage includes appointments with your assigned doctor, as well as consultations with an RDN. However, because Gennev accepts insurance, your prescriptions will likely require a co-pay.

While the Gennev platform doesn’t list exactly what medications are available, there is mention of FDA-approved hormonal medications, nonhormonal ones, and supplements for menopause.

Overall, Gennev may be worth a shot if you have an accepted healthcare plan. Otherwise, you might be better off with Winona, Alloy, or Evernow.

Alloy

Unlike Winona, Alloy offers more than just treatments for menopause symptoms; it also includes options for skin care (like tretinoin), gut health (a synbiotic), and weight loss, including prescription medications such as Wegovy.

In terms of its menopause therapies, many of Alloy’s offerings are similar to the estrogen and progesterone treatments available through Winona, often at a lower monthly cost (though Alloy typically requires a three-month commitment). Alloy also provides a few routes of administration that Winona doesn’t, including an estrogen spray and gel.

Beyond hormone therapy, Alloy offers options like low-dose birth control pills and a nonhormonal treatment for hot flashes (paroxetine), which may appeal to those looking for alternatives to traditional HRT. Like Winona, Alloy will provide you with the necessary documentation to submit for HSA/FSA reimbursement.

Evernow

Among the competitors mentioned here, Evernow is the only one that requires a paid membership in addition to medication costs. While this adds complexity compared to other services, its overall pricing has become more competitive in recent months, and the company has begun accepting some insurance plans, though coverage remains limited and situation-dependent.

Here’s how Evernow’s pricing works:

Membership plans: start at about $49 per month, with discounted options like $129 for three months (about $43/month) and $420 for a year (about $35/month)

One-time visits: available for around $150 per appointment and may be covered by insurance, depending on your plan

Medication costs: charged separately, with tablets around $27.50 per month and patches ranging from about $59.99 to $89.99 for a 30-day supply

Insurance usage: can be applied to medications only if they’re filled through a local pharmacy

Home delivery: prescriptions shipped directly to your home are typically cash-pay

Membership coverage: membership fees are not covered by insurance

HSA/FSA: can be used for membership costs, but not for delivered medications

With this structure, Evernow can be competitive with — or even less expensive than — other providers in some cases, particularly for tablet-based treatments. However, total costs still depend on how you choose to access care, whether you use insurance, and how prescriptions are filled.

The available treatments for menopause symptoms are similar to those from Alloy and Winona, with the addition of vaginal estradiol tablets and a few nonhormonal options (Femistry, Veozah, and paroxetine).

Like Alloy, Evernow also offers treatments outside of menopause care, including hair-loss options such as oral minoxidil and a topical finasteride/minoxidil solution. However, the selection is relatively limited, and there are often more cost-effective providers with broader offerings in this category, such as Hers.

Winona FAQ

Is Winona HRT legit?

Yes, Winona is a legitimate service that employs board-certified doctors who can prescribe you HRT treatments, including estrogen and combination therapy.

Does Winona accept insurance?

No, Winona does not currently accept insurance. However, you can use your HSA/FSA at checkout or submit for reimbursement.

Is Winona HRT FDA-approved?

Winona’s compounded treatments are not FDA-approved (no compounded medication is). However, the estrogen patch, estrogen tablets, and progesterone capsules are not compounded and are FDA-approved treatments.

How long does Winona take to work?

The timeline for seeing results from Winona’s treatments depends on the type of therapy and the symptoms being treated. HRT can begin improving symptoms like hot flashes and night sweats within a few weeks, though it may take several months to experience the full benefits.5 Other treatments, such as DHEA or topical sildenafil, may have more variable timelines and less consistent results. As with any hormone-based therapy, individual response can vary.

Do you need a prescription to use Winona?

Yes, most of Winona’s treatments require a prescription from a licensed medical provider. After you complete the online intake questionnaire, a physician reviews your information and determines whether HRT or another treatment is appropriate. Some options, like DHEA, are available over the counter in the United States, but Winona still includes them as part of a provider-guided treatment plan to help ensure safe and appropriate use.7
