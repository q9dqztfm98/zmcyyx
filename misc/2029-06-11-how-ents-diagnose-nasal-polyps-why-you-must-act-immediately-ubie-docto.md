Explanation

Nasal polyps are soft, benign (noncancerous) growths that form on the lining of your nasal passages or sinuses. They can lead to a blocked nose, reduced sense of smell, and recurring sinus infections. If you suspect you have nasal polyps, understanding how ENT doctors diagnose polyps in the nose can help you feel more prepared and less anxious about the process.

What Are Nasal Polyps?

Appearance: Clusters of inflamed tissue that look like small grapes or teardrops.

Common areas: Inside the nasal cavities and the sinuses.

Symptoms: Nasal congestion, runny nose, facial pain or pressure, snoring, and a diminished sense of smell or taste.

Nasal polyps develop when chronic inflammation causes the nasal lining to swell and form tissue growths. Untreated, they may grow and further block your airways, affecting sleep and daily activities.

Why Accurate Diagnosis Matters

Getting the right diagnosis is crucial because:

It rules out more serious conditions (e.g., nasal tumors).

It guides targeted treatment—medication or surgery.

It helps manage underlying contributors such as allergies, asthma, or infections.

It reduces the risk of complications like chronic sinusitis.

Knowing what to expect during an ENT visit can ease concerns and empower you to ask the right questions.

How Do ENT Doctors Diagnose Polyps in the Nose?

ENT (ear, nose, and throat) specialists use a step-by-step approach to confirm nasal polyps. Here's what typically happens:

1. Detailed Medical History

Your doctor will ask about:

Symptoms: Onset, duration, triggers, and severity of nasal blockage, pressure, or loss of smell.

Personal and family history: Allergies, asthma, aspirin sensitivity (Samter's triad), and past sinus infections.

Medication use: Over-the-counter nasal sprays, decongestants, or steroid treatments and their effectiveness.

Environmental factors: Exposure to irritants like smoke, dust, or strong perfumes.

Understanding your history helps your doctor identify risk factors and tailor the exam.

2. Physical Examination

A basic nose exam may include:

Anterior rhinoscopy: Using a speculum and light to look into the front of your nose.

Palpation: Gently pressing around the sinuses (forehead, cheeks) to check for tenderness or pressure.

Nasal airflow tests: Assessing how well air moves through your nostrils.

While this exam can suggest polyps, more detailed visualization is often needed.

3. Nasal Endoscopy

A nasal endoscopy offers a clear, close-up view:

What it is: A thin, flexible tube with a camera (endoscope) inserted through the nostril.

Why it helps: It allows the ENT to see deep into the nasal passages and sinuses, identify the size and location of polyps, and rule out other issues.

Comfort: Local anesthetic or decongestant spray is applied first. You may feel mild pressure but generally no pain.

This is the most direct way to confirm nasal polyps and assess surrounding inflammation.

4. Imaging Tests

If your polyps are large, recurrent, or you have chronic sinusitis, imaging may be ordered:

Computed tomography (CT) scan: Provides detailed cross-sectional images of your sinuses and nasal cavities.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): Rarely used but helpful if your doctor suspects an unusual growth or needs to distinguish soft tissues.

These scans map out the extent of disease, guide surgery planning, and help identify sinus blockages.

5. Allergy and Blood Tests

Since allergies often contribute to chronic inflammation, your ENT may recommend:

Skin prick tests: To identify specific allergens (pollen, dust mites, pet dander).

Blood tests: To measure overall allergic response (IgE levels) or check for conditions like cystic fibrosis in younger patients.

Treating allergies alongside polyps reduces recurrence and improves outcomes.

6. Biopsy (Rare Cases)

If a growth looks unusual or doesn't respond to standard treatment, a small tissue sample (biopsy) may be taken:

Procedure: Under local anesthesia, a tiny piece of tissue is removed through the nostril.

Purpose: To rule out malignancy or other uncommon conditions.

Aftercare: Minimal discomfort; some mild bleeding is normal.

Most nasal polyps are benign and don't require biopsy.

What to Expect After Diagnosis

Once your ENT confirms nasal polyps, the focus shifts to managing symptoms, reducing inflammation, and preventing recurrence. Here's what comes next:

1. Medical Treatment

Topical corticosteroids: Nasal sprays (e.g., fluticasone, mometasone) to shrink polyps and ease congestion.

Oral steroids: Short courses (e.g., prednisone) for more severe cases. Effective but used carefully due to side effects.

Saline rinses: At-home sinus irrigation to keep nasal passages moist and clear of irritants.

Antihistamines or allergy shots: If allergies play a major role in your inflammation.

These treatments often reduce polyp size, improve breathing, and delay or prevent surgery.

2. Surgical Options

If medication alone isn't enough, surgery may be recommended:

Endoscopic sinus surgery: The most common procedure. The surgeon uses an endoscope to remove polyps and open sinus drainage pathways.

Polypectomy: Removal of polyps with a small suction device or microdebrider.

Recovery: Usually outpatient, with nasal packing or dissolvable strips. Mild discomfort, swelling, and nasal drainage are normal for a few days.

Surgery improves airflow and sinus drainage, but controlling underlying inflammation is key to long-term success.

3. Ongoing Monitoring

Long-term follow-up helps spot recurrences early:

Regular nasal endoscopies (every 6–12 months) if you have severe or recurrent polyps.

Adjustments in medication based on symptoms.

Continued allergy management and environmental controls (e.g., air purifiers, dust-mite–proof bedding).

Tips for Managing Expectations

Healing takes time. Steroid sprays can take several weeks to work.

Even after surgery, you may need periodic steroid treatments or allergy care.

Communicate openly with your ENT about side effects or new symptoms.

Keep a symptom diary (nasal blockage, pressure, smell changes) to track progress.

When to Seek Immediate Care

While polyps themselves aren't life-threatening, certain signs warrant prompt medical attention:

Sudden high fever, severe headache, or stiff neck.

Vision changes or swelling around the eyes.

Deep facial pain not relieved by usual treatments.
