# The True Story of the Quad-Blasting Viral Exercise. Here's How to Use the Somersault Squat.

A FEW MONTHS ago, Joe Daniels’s notifications started blowing up after trainers, fitness influencers, and everyday gymgoers began pointing their butts to the sky.

Names like IFBB Pro Petar Klancir, Julian Zaletti, and Bret Contreras were posting instructions and commentary about a “new” exercise that Daniels had pioneered: The somersault squat. Except the move, a bent-forward, front-loaded squat variation that isolates (and smashes) the quadriceps, isn’t exactly new. Daniels came up with it more than 15 years ago, when he was an amateur bodybuilder competing for titles like the 2009 Cincinnati NPC Championship (which he won).

After stumbling onto the move while performing a weighted stretch on a Smith machine, Daniels posted about the discovery on a still relatively young video website called “YouTube.” Every few years since then, he says, he’s gotten notifications that the video is blowing up as people discover (and re-discover) perhaps the most quad-focused squatting variation out there.

“People were looking for ‘how can I train my legs at home’ [during COVID lockdowns,] and the somersault squat with the kettlebell kind of boosted up there,” says the now-45 year old Daniels, who has focused on kettlebell-oriented muscle gain for more than a decade at KBMuscle.com. “And it just blew up [again] in the past six months or so. I’m seeing all these trainers say, ‘I found this thing called the somersault squat. It looks silly. It looks insane and ridiculous, but I tried it, and here’s what’s happened.’”

What “happened,” usually, is that their quads got a serious pump. That’s what has made Men’s Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S., so hyped about this move. One of his favorite gyms doesn’t have a leg extension machine, but Samuel still wants to hammer the fronts of his thighs. The bent-over position of the somersault almost completely takes the hamstrings and glutes out of the equation, letting him build his quads without the machine.

Why the Somersault Squat Works, and Why We Love It

Daniels stumbled across the quad-killing power of the somersault squat while he was stuck under the bar of a Smith machine. He’d placed the bar deep within the crease of his hip and bent at the knees, giving his hamstrings a weighted stretch.

“I thought, ‘okay, now I need to get up. I have to get out of this thing.’ So I just tilted the bar forward, took the locks off, and I kind of extended my quad because there was no other way to do it,” he says. “When I came up, I was like, ‘wait, that feels really interesting!’”

The weight had been dialed in almost completely on his quads. He did a few more reps with the 90 pounds that were loaded on the bar, and could barely walk afterwards. After a few days of tinkering, and thinking of how the move looked almost like you’d tumble forward into a somersault, he posted a video and gave the move its name.

Somersault squats’ quad-focused power comes from that bent-over posture, Samuel explains.

“When we squat, there are multiple drivers to the motion, and we use a lot of our hamstrings and glutes,” he says. But with your torso bent over, you can focus on making sure knees go in front of your toes during the somersault. You also focus on your quads when you straighten your legs. “Because of that, the hamstrings and glutes are less involved.”

This isn’t just a benefit for guys who are trying to create quad hypertrophy to flash at bodybuilding shows and shorts with a five-inch inseam. As Daniels transitioned from bodybuilding to kettlebell-focused training, including kettlebell sport competitions, he kept somersaulting; the move, he says, helped him build the strength-endurance in his quads he needed to launch himself into jerk after jerk after jerk in the 10-plus-minute sets of competition kettlebell work.

He also found it worked for his clients with back pain and other issues that make conventionally loaded lower body training difficult. “Those people think they can’t squat at all, so they can’t train their legs,” he says. “With the somersault squat, you can train your quads without having a high systemic load on your body [and spine].”

Yeah, it looks kind of silly. Daniels says that some detractors have derisively referred to his creation as a “stripper squat,” or “10 rounds of ass to the sky.” But “embarrassing” looking moves can often be the most effective: The hip thrust, once ridiculed as a “sky hump,” is now well-known as one of the weight room’s most powerful moves for glute gains.

Samuel and Daniels say there are reasons to brave a few funny looks and embrace the suck of the somersault:

Work the Quads With Minimal Equipment

Samuel’s no-leg-extension gym isn’t unique. The trend in gym-building over the 2010s, he says, was away from machines, and towards more free weights. And while there are enough isolation moves for other body parts (think: curls for biceps and skullcrushers for triceps), targeting your quads alone could be tough. The somersault squat, with its back-friendly, hamstring-hampering position, puts your other muscles at an anatomical disadvantage, putting more stress on the quads … even if you’re dealing with lighter weights at home.

Blast the Quads With High Volume

Quads, like triceps, benefit from some high-rep work, Daniels says. But, you might ask, instead of bending over and looking silly, why not just squat a lot? “When we squat, we spend a lot of time thinking about our upper body, because we we need to do that to stay safe,” says Samuel. “Because of that, we can’t really take things to complete fatigue in a high-volume way. But you can do [high volume] with [the somersault] squat … because of the placement of the load.”

This Exercise Hits Different

Most guys feel the leg extension machine in the vastus medialis, the teardrop of the thigh that hugs the inside of the knee. But when they try the somersault squat for the first time, Daniels says, trainees remark on how the move taxes the vastus lateralis, the meaty outside of the quad which usually gets overpowered in the leg extension.

Why Certain Guys Should Not Do the Somersault Squat

Somersault squats’ extreme position makes them iffy for guys with existing knee injuries or pain, Daniels says. If you’ve had knee surgery or have recurring knee issues, skip them.

There’s also a move you need to understand before you do the somersault squat, says Samuel. The goblet squat teaches basic squat mechanics, and, at the very least, you should understand how to do that move. Goblet squats also help your body start to understand how to move knee over toe in a safe manner.

“Before you move your knee over your toe, you have to understand how to squat with a vertical shin,” he says. “If you can’t goblet squat at least half your bodyweight for 8 to 10 reps, then you probably shouldn’t be focusing on getting your knee over your toe with load in the somersault squat position.”

How to Do the Somersault Squat with Proper Form

To do the somersault squat, you'll need some platform to elevate you feet (stacked plates or, ideally, a slant board) and a kettlebell.

Start with your feet shoulder- to hip-width apart on the floor, or with your heels slightly elevated on a pair of small weight plates or a slant board. Set the kettlebell between your feet so that the handle is parallel to your legs, bisecting your body.

Place your forearms deep into the creases of your hips. Squat down with your arms in this position so that your hands reach the kettlebell’s handle, and your hamstrings are on the backs of your calves. Grab the handle of the kettlebell.

Push back with your legs so your torso comes forward as your butt goes up and back. At the top of this position, you’ll basically be at the bent-over position of a good morning or stiff-legged deadlift exercise.

These form tips can help fine-tune the move:

Start Light

Warm up sets are always a good idea for getting your joints moving, but with the somersault squat, it can also help you get used to an unusual position, Samuel says.

Start without about half as much weight as you think you’ll work with, and get into both the bottom and top of the exercise position. “I try to live in the bottom position for a second,” he says. “That’s what’s going to get you comfortable in that bottom range of motion. Hold for a split second at the bottom.”

Keep the Weight as High on Your Femur as Possible

When you’re doing somersault squats, you want to keep the weight under your hips as much as possible to keep the tension on your quads, and off your joints, Daniels says. When the weight slides down the femur too much, the pressure’s off your thigh muscles and onto your skeletal system, which can cause stress on the knees.

Don’t Lock Out at the Top

Completely straightening your knees at the top takes the tension off the quads, Daniels says, making the exercise less intense. But it also puts a massive stretch on your hamstrings. That’s great, but it’s not what you’re training with this isolation move. Limit your range of motion slightly to help tax (and tire out) the quads.

Turn Up the Intensity with a Spanish Somersault Squat

If somersault squats have one weakness as a quad builder, it’s that there’s little tension at the top of the move, Samuel says. To round out their quad-smashing ability for a more advanced move, he suggests adding a band and combining it with another quad-focused move—the Spanish squat.

Often used in rehab settings for ACL reconstruction and patellar tendinitis, Spanish squats are performed with a resistance band wrapped around the insides of the knees, and then anchored to a pole or weight machine in front of the legs. This lets the Spanish squatter sit back into the move, as the band pulls the legs forward.

Setting up a somersault squat with a band wrapped in the Spanish squat position gives the quads a one-two punch, Samuel says.

“Every time you try to straighten out your knees with those bands, there’s direct force trying to pull them forward. And what that does is forces your quads to contract really aggressively as you’re starting to straighten,” he says. “We have tension in the stretch position [from the band], and we have tension in the shortened position [from the somersault squat maneuver.]”

Be warned: Daniels says this variation is extremely intense. And Samuel says the setup is tough to get hang of.

How to Incorporate the Somersault Squat in Your Workouts

Since it’s an isolation exercise, somersault squats should act as an accessory move to compound exercises that tax more than the quads, Samuel says. That means doing somersaults near the end of your workout, after you’ve already done your more all-around squats, deadlifts, or lunges.

“I’m using it at the end of a leg day. I’ll get my heavy moves in first, like very heavy deadlifts, heavy walking lunges,” he says. “And then I’ll do somersault squats as either my third or fourth exercise.”

Putting somersault squats this late in the workout takes advantage of the fact that they’re harder to do with heavier weights, he says. Your quads are already somewhat taxed from the other moves, so you can use lighter loads and still take the quads near failure in the somersaults.

To get near failure in this way, Daniels and Samuel both suggest longer sets with more reps—12 to 20 reps in each set.

This, Daniels says, plays into how our quads work.

“There’s a lot of endurance there, just like the triceps. It’s the same thing, joint extension,” he says. “With the somersault squats, if I’m doing sets of five, it has to be really heavy, but it’s not that same feeling [of quad burn]. So I tend to do them for higher reps.”
