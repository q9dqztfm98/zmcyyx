Checklist item #1: A goal Make your goal something that provides a tangible measure-not "I want to get in shape," but "I want to drop from a 38-inch waist to a 36." For parents of small children, the goal can even be something as simple as "I want to find 15 minutes each day for family activity." Then write it down. "Take the time and energy to commit your goal to paper, otherwise it's just more of a wish or something you hope happens," Staley says. Finally, bolster the goal by listing three ways your life or the lives of your children will improve if you meet it.

Checklist item #2: Competitive spirit "Competing with oneself is excellent for encouraging a kid to become involved in an activity," says Rimm. Pounds don't immediately leap up and evacuate the body. It takes time to notice results. Keeping a log of personal records helps connect effort and outcome. It works for adults, too. "Establish and always try to beat your personal record for each exercise--this helps keep you emotionally invested in reaching your goal," Staley says. "Otherwise, you're just burning calories, and how boring is that?"

Checklist item #3: The right mindset Your child may need to lose a few pounds, but exercise shouldn't become unduly focused on fighting fat. "Try to emphasize good health rather than weight loss," says Rimm. A too-rigid approach can have the opposite effect, driving kids into body-image problems.

Checklist item #4: Your own unwavering commitment to the cause "If you don't consider fitness a priority, your kids won't put much value in it, either," says Michael Mejia, M.S., C.S.C.S, advisor to the Center for Sports Parenting. "Kids are sponges, and parental influence is huge." Do as you say, and they'll do as you do.
