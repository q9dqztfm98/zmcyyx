# How to Clear the Air

I would like to think I'm not just as environmentally conscious as the next person but more so. I take pride in doing my part to keep the air clean by driving an energy-efficient car; using hand-powered tools, such as rakes and clippers, in the yard; and cooling my family's house with a power-conserving attic fan—in sharp contrast to my unenlightened neighbors who drive huge pickups or SUVs, fire up leaf blowers, and blast the air-conditioning in their homes. But I keep quiet about it. Paragons like me have to lead by example. (To find out how you can help reduce pollution, check out "Sign-up to Save the World")

Unfortunately, self-righteousness has a way of crumbling under scrutiny, which for me came after I learned a few more things about air quality. My car, for starters, is green, but only its color. According to the EPA, the air pollution score for my 2003 Subaru wagon is slightly below average. (Check your car's score at Green Vehicle Guide.) Worse, in the winter I sometimes start the car and let it idle so it's nice and toasty for my wife and kids, and that puts 20 times more pollution into the air than driving at 30 miles per hour. Nice and toasty appears to be one of my downfalls; I also installed a wood-burning stove back in 1999, supposedly as a Y2K precaution but really because burning wood seems so cozy and "natural." Yet wood-burning stoves are a major source of particulate contaminants.

The more research I did, the more I found the world of air pollution to be full of surprises. Two hours southwest of my home is the town of Lancaster, Pennsylvania—famous for its surrounding agrarian beauty and horse-and-buggy Amish community but among the 25 worst cities for air pollution in the United States, according to the American Lung Association. That's not an aberration. "I was stunned to find Knoxville, and some other cities in the Southeast, where I'm from, that had air pollution levels like New York's," says Janice Nolen, director of national policy for the ALA and author of its annual State of the Air Report. "People aren't aware that this isn't just a big-city problem."

In fact, more than 52 percent of Americans live in counties that have unhealthy levels of some kind of air pollution, typically concentrated in large metropolitan areas or, like Lancaster, in regions downwind from major industrial pollution belts, according to the ALA's 2005 report. By EPA estimates, as many as 50,000 Americans die prematurely every year from conditions that dirty air causes or makes worse. "That's an extraordinary toll on health," says John Walke, clean air director at the Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC). "People are concerned, but the level of worry doesn't match the magnitude of the problem."

The bottom line is that the air in your community may be dirtier than you ever imagined. Here's what you need to know to protect your family's health.

Air pollution is actually two distinct evils. One is soot, or particulate matter (PM). The other is ground-level ozone, a gaseous pollutant that's prevalent today, thanks largely to gases from dirty electric power plants and cars. (Among experts, smog has come to mean ozone.) For ways to clear the air, check out "10 Ways to Make Your Air Cleaner".

Ozone doesn't spew directly from tailpipes and smokestacks but is more like a weather condition. "It forms when you get different elements in the right proportions," Nolen says. The recipe for the perfect ozone calls for nitrogen oxides (combinations of oxygen and nitrogen produced when gasoline and other fuels burn) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which are vaporous chemicals or fumes from sources such as gasoline, paint, dry-cleaning chemicals, and industrial emissions. Factories are one of the biggest sources of VOCs (providing 45 percent of the total), but they contribute about five percent of nitrogen oxides (NOx). The rest of the NOx comes mostly from burning fuel in electric power plants, homes, and offices (nearly 40?percent), and from vehicle exhaust (about 55 percent). In addition to VOCs and NOx, however, making ozone demands sunlight and heat, which cooks the noxious stuff in the air and changes its chemical composition. That means ozone cannot form at night and that it's more prevalent in warm, bright places, such as Southern California, than in colder, dimmer areas, such as northern Minnesota. It also means the worst ozone follows seasonal or weather patterns, becoming more common in summer, like tornadoes.

There are smog (or ozone) "alleys" in various parts of the country, where local landscape and wind patterns combine to make the air worse. Areas surrounded by mountains, such as the city of Denver, suffer because the terrain traps in ozone. In the Northeast, vast regions are polluted by drift carried on prevailing winds from coal-fired power plants that may be hundreds of miles away—some as far as the Midwest. Other trouble spots are found in parts of the Southeast, such as the hot, stagnant areas surrounding Atlanta and Nashville; in the industrial Lake Michigan area, including Chicago and Milwaukee; and in large areas of oil-refinery country in southeastern Texas, near Houston. "Even bucolic towns get massive amounts of air pollution, which is out of their control, from states to their west," Walke says. "Lancaster is bad because it's getting pollution from western Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and the Ohio River Valley, which is home to the biggest coal plants on the planet." Even the rugged beauty of Acadia National Park in Maine is clouded by dirty air: "It's at the end of the national tailpipe," he explains.

Particulate matter, which also travels on the winds, is a stew of microscopic bits of dust, soot, and water droplets in the air, along with substances formed through chemical reactions. It comes from cars, power plants, and factories, although diesel exhaust and dusty clouds from construction sites, unpaved roads, and plowed open fields also contribute.

Despite all the problems, our air is far better today than it was during the past three decades. In 1970, the Clean Air Act was signed into law after catastrophic episodes of smog and soot pollution left scores of people dead. In one 1966 incident, for instance, 150 New Yorkers died from respiratory failure and heart disease when weather patterns kept an intense smog in place over the city during the Thanksgiving holiday. The act gave the EPA the authority to set national air quality standards and force polluters to meet them. "The Clean Air Act is one of the most effective pieces of legislation ever written," Walke says. All types of air pollution are being reduced across the country, he adds, except in such areas as Atlanta and Phoenix, where extensive population growth has made air quality worse.

"Collectively, we've cut air pollutants by more than half since 1970," says Jeffrey Holmstead, assistant administrator of the EPA's Office of Air and Radiation. "In that same period, the economy grew by nearly 200 percent, miles traveled by cars and trucks climbed by more than 170 percent, and energy consumption rose almost 50 percent. We have more to do, but it's important to understand that we've made enormous progress."

Recent studies, including newly published research commissioned by the EPA, show that even though there's been substantial progress in the past 35 years, the damage caused by air pollution is still severe and wide-ranging. A study of 95 U.S. cities published in 2004 by the Journal of the American Medical Association linked ozone to premature deaths from such problems as respiratory and heart conditions. The researchers found that an increase in ozone—although well below the current regulatory standards for clean air—caused 3,767 deaths, a number they say is probably an underestimate.

Ozone has long been connected to increased risks from respiratory diseases such as emphysema, bronchitis, and especially asthma. "About a day after [a bout of bad air], you see more visits to the ER, more prescriptions for medication, and other signs of asthma activity, in both adults and children," says David Peden, MD, director of the Center for Environmental Medicine, Asthma, and Lung Biology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. One 2005 study conducted at Emory University in Atlanta found that air pollution spiked ER visits for respiratory problems by about three percent. "Whether air pollution actually causes asthma is not as clear-cut as you may think," Peden says, "but it definitely makes asthma more active." Ozone irritates the airways and causes inflammation that can make it harder to draw air into the lungs; it can also make asthma attacks become worse. "There's evidence that air pollution can trigger a direct reflex of the sensory nerves, which makes it more difficult to breathe," Peden says, even for those who don't have asthma.

Particulate matter is also harmful to our health: One of the hottest new areas of research focuses on how it affects the heart. "The particles we're most concerned about are a fraction of the size of a red blood cell, so they can get deep into the lungs—and there's some evidence they pass from there into other parts of the body," says Norman Edelman, MD, the ALA's chief medical officer. "Some data suggests particulate matter may be causing more deaths than ozone." In 2004 researchers at Brigham Young University, in Provo, Utah, found a strong link between long-term exposure to PM and an eight to 18 percent higher risk of dying from cardiovascular problems, such as pulmonary inflammation, accelerated atherosclerosis, and cardiac arrest.

Doctors have found evidence that fine PM raises risks of lung cancer, according to a study of 500,000 people published by the Journal of the American Medical Association in 2002. And research from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences earlier this year showed that exposure during pregnancy to forms of pollution caused by vehicle exhaust, heating systems, and tobacco smoking may cause chromosomal damage, which can increase the risk of cancer in children. Kids are especially vulnerable to the harmful effects of mercury, an ingredient of power plant emissions that is carried through the air, settles in water, and can damage the nervous systems of those who eat contaminated fish. "Air pollution is a pervasive problem that has a sweeping impact on public health," says John Walke of the NRDC.

As dirty as outdoor air is, you may face an even bigger threat from the air in your home and office. "That's where Americans spend 90 percent of their time," says Jeffrey Holmstead of the EPA. In fact, air pollution levels in your house can be two to five times higher—and sometimes as much as 100 times higher—than those outside. Yet many Americans don't know that the EPA ranks indoor air quality among its top four environmental health concerns.

Why is indoor air so bad? Partly because homes and office buildings get little ventilation. As a result, "it takes only a low indoor pollutant emission rate to significantly degrade indoor air quality," says William Fisk, senior scientist and head of the Indoor Environment Department at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, in Berkeley, California. "The risks can be substantial." Below are the most serious indoor pollutants—and how to steer clear of them.

At Home

RADON An odorless, colorless, radioactive gas that comes naturally from uranium in the soil, radon is suspected by the EPA of causing as many as 30,000 deaths a year from lung cancer. To get referrals to contractors who can test your home, contact your state's radon office (find it at Indoor Air Quality). If you have a radon problem, the contractor can install a venting system.

CARBON MONOXIDE This odorless gas is emitted by kerosene or gas space heaters, wood-burning stoves, fire-places, furnaces, water heaters, and gas cooktops; it can cause headaches, dizziness, nausea, and—at high levels—even death. To help prevent carbon monoxide from accumulating, make sure your heating equipment, stoves, and fireplaces are vented to the outside and inspected every year. Install carbon monoxide detectors in your basement or wherever your heating system is located, and in your home's living area.

PBDEs Polybrominated diphenylethers are chemicals used as flame retardants in such diverse items as children's sleepwear, upholstered furniture, mattresses, computers, and office equipment. They can be released into the air as fine particles of dust that settle in your home or workplace. Recent studies have found high concentrations of pbdes in adults, newborns, and breast milk. While no research has been done on humans thus far, findings from animal studies suggest that PDBEs may have a wide range of toxic health effects, including disrupted brain development, impaired immunity, and possibly cancer. The best way to avoid these chemicals is to purchase furnishings and electronics that do not contain them. For example, Ikea, Sony, and Toshiba have already phased PDBEs out of their products.

HOUSEHOLD CLEANERS AND DISINFECTANTS A less obvious source of indoor air pollution, maybe because they often smell so fresh, these products contain organic chemicals—compounds that can irritate the eyes, nose, and throat; trigger headaches; and cause nausea. Whenever possible, use nontoxic cleaners and/or natural solutions: vinegar for disinfecting, lemon juice for wiping down fixtures, and baking soda for scrubbing. If you must use conventional cleaners, make sure rooms are ventilated with fresh air.

PAINT More hazardous sources of organic chemicals than household cleaners and disinfectants, paints, paint strippers, and varnish removers may contain the possible carcinogens benzene and methylene chloride. Check labels for warnings and use products containing these chemicals only outside or in well-ventilated areas. Better yet, use low-VOC paints, such as the Pristine Eco Spec line from Benjamin Moore.

FORMALDEHYDE Appliances that burn fuel (such as heaters and ovens), household cleansers, and some paints also contain small amounts of this chemical. It can cause irritation, along with allergic reactions, asthma attacks, and possibly cancer, according to the EPA. The biggest source of formaldehyde in most homes, however, is furniture and building materials, such as the adhesives that bind the pressed wood in particleboard. Usually these products give off the most formaldehyde when they're new, so vent rooms with fresh air as long as you detect a chemical odor. If the air is uncomfortably warm or humid, run the air conditioner—heat accelerates the release of formaldehyde into the air.

At Work

If you spend all day in an office, you face many of the same indoor air pollutants you do at home, though some may be worse. For example, you may be exposed to more PDBEs if you sit in a closed room full of computers and office equipment. The biggest difference between an office and a home, however, is that offices are more likely to be sealed, with air quality controlled through large ventilation systems. "The main problems are usually moisture and dirt collection in the system, and workspaces not getting enough ventilation," says Mark Mendell, PhD, an epidemiologist in the Indoor Environment Department at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. These conditions encourage molds and bacteria to grow, which can trigger allergy and asthma attacks. Report any leaks from plumbing, windows, or roofs immediately (the flat roofs typical of office buildings are often prone to leaks). If you spill a liquid, clean it up right away. Store food in refrigerators, and remove garbage promptly so you don't attract pests that could produce allergens or make pesticides necessary. Never block air vents with furniture, boxes, or piles of paper. And don't count on houseplants to help clean the air. "They're too small to make a difference as air scrubbers and may actually encourage growth of molds," says Janice Nolen of the ALA.

Finally, at work and at home, open your windows. Increasing ventilation indoors is one of the best ways to disperse chemical pollutants, accelerate the venting of combustion by-products, and discourage mold growth. Ahh! Now you can breathe easier.

In March, the Bush administration instituted several new pollution rules that the EPA says "will make the next 15 years one of the most productive periods of air quality improvement in America's history." By gradually capping emissions of such pollutants as nitrogen oxides through 2015, the Clean Air Interstate Rule will reduce the palls drifting from coal-fired power plants in 28 states in the eastern half of the United States. (The West relies on cleaner fuels, such as natural gas.) Another rule regulates mercury emissions from coal-fired plants.

Some groups, such as the NRDC, charge that the new regulations don't do enough to curb pollution, and in certain cases may actually encourage it. They are especially concerned about changes to the Clean Air Act that allow old power plants to avoid meeting new pollution standards when they do upgrades costing less than 20 percent of the plant's value. Loosening the upgrade requirements "essentially means some plants never have to meet new standards," Janice Nolen says. "It carves a huge hole in the rules." The administration says the new upgrade rules motivate plant owners to modernize and give them more leeway in deciding for themselves how they'll contribute to state and national emissions goals. Environmental groups sued the government to keep the stricter regulations in place, but in June, a federal appeals court ruled in the administration's favor.

Another point of contention is how emissions are reduced. A credit- trading system allows plants that do more cleanup than the EPA requires to earn pollution credits that can be traded or sold to other plants. In effect, the right to pollute becomes a commodity that a power plant can buy if it's dirty—or profit from if it's clean.

Trading lets too many polluters off the hook, Nolen says. "Even if you reduce overall emissions on average, you're not breathing that average if you're downwind from a dirty plant—you're breathing what comes out of that plant," she points out. "We're glad to have tons of pollutants out of the air, but some people are paying a price they shouldn't have to because companies don't want to clean up every plant."
