# First Blood

"Double left jab!" Doug DeWitt barked. I prodded at a pad attached to his hand.

Thwock! Thwock!

"C'mon! Snap it out!"

"Hands up!"

"Rocket Man!" wailed Elton John, fortissimo.

I was halfway to exhaustion in the middle of Scarsdale, New York, at DeWitt's private boxing gym. There were framed press clippings and photos of giants—Roberto Duran, Thomas "Hit Man" Hearns—on the wall. Another guy and three women were at my sides, punching DeWitt's pads in turn, then shadowboxing to classic rock. DeWitt, a former middleweight champion, worked along the row until he was once more in my face, assessing me coolly from under hooded lids with eyes as hard as marbles.

"Right hook!" he instructed.

Thwock!

My arms felt like lengths of damp towel, but he was remorseless. "Try to take his head off. That's what I always wanted to do," he said. He threw a left hook to demonstrate; the oncoming punch generated a force field as it stopped a centimeter or so from my jaw.

"You're going to be positive," DeWitt said. "You're going to look good. You're not going to embarrass yourself. You want to win, right?" The Scarsdale women looked at me, clearly baffled.

He meant my Big Fight.

It began so: Three years ago I attended a party in downtown Manhattan at which the entertainment included a boxing ring. Fueled by white wine and egged on by bystanders, I decided to spar a few rounds. The last time I'd put on gloves, I had been about 10. I had been bopped on the nose, hadn't liked it one bit, and that had been that. Now in the ring I had a curious feeling—that this was a terrain I could tread, a language I could learn. I connected fairly often, withstood a few punches, and won by a technical knockout in the third round. Dumb beginner's luck. I ran into David Leslie not long afterward. Leslie, a performance artist and keen boxer, urged me to fight again, for real this time.

The timing was excellent. I was in a stew—at the end of an affair, flailing in a dozen directions at work—and the thought of losing myself in something so very different was bracing. "The ring is another country," Leslie said in a mystical moment. A slightly scary country, of course. But I was in fair shape for somebody over 60 and I didn't feel the project would be "insane"—the favorite, worried adjective applied to it by friends. So I got in touch with a boxer I had met, Doug DeWitt.

DeWitt's gym is deep in Commute Country; I caught the train out from New York City. Here he works with both men and women—the men a mixed bunch, the women often with previous lives as corporate lawyers and the like, now married and pounding out their aggressions with their fists. Classes would begin the same way. DeWitt would set the timer. We would line up facing the mirror and skip rope for a couple of 3-minute rounds. Then the wall bags, the air bag, the light bag (which rises from the floor on a springy column and is the size of a head), the speed bag, the heavy bag. Then DeWitt would slip on the pads and command left jabs, right crosses, left and right hooks, and uppercuts. Hand weights meant the 30 minutes was reaching its end. Finally, the abs. At the beginning, the rope skipping made sport of my wind, the wall bags punished my stamina, and the light bag mocked my reflexes.

I trained five or six times a week for 2 months. I found a jump rope of a weight I could use; I came to enjoy the rhythm of the wall bags and the creaking mass of the heavy bag. In the third week, I was working on combinations. By the fourth week, I was sparring. In the fifth week, I elected to try full-contact sparring, including punches to the head (wearing headgear, of course). First time out, my opponent caught me repeatedly on both sides of my head, annoyingly but not too hard. My own punches seemed ineffective—just tentative prods and pushes. But when my opponent took off his headpiece, he had blood on his forehead.

My first "real" fight was at Gleason's, a famous boxing gym on the Brooklyn waterfront. My opponent was Phil Maier, an affable New York City judge in his mid-40s. It would be a "white-collar" fight—no winner would be decided. Still, both DeWitt and David Leslie warned me that I might freeze in the ring. Apparently, novices—however they've been performing in the gym—often become paralyzed by nerves in their first fight.

I didn't freeze.

I felt cool and affectless, as though I were doing a job that needed to be done. After three rounds of civilized prodding and poking, Judge Phil and I tapped gloves and politely parted ways. For a fight, it was a pretty relaxed affair.

My next match would be more intense.

David Leslie's nickname is "The Impact Addict." He has richly earned it. He does a show entitled Box Opera, which combines boxing and performance art. His own boxing is serious—he went three rounds with the world champion Riddick Bowe in a match on the Staten Island ferry—but it is also performance. "Your music is very important," he says. "And what's written on your pants. And the gloves." He was, he told me, planning another Box Opera. He would himself be fighting the huge heavyweight Gerry Cooney. He would be boxing all-out, but expected to be knocked out. Not expected, hoped. "This will be my last fight," he said.

He asked me to fight one of the bouts. I find I normally say yes to things. So I did again.

I left the choice of an opponent to Leslie, and it turned out that this, my third match, was actually a rematch. After my wine-fueled fight 2 years back, I learned that my opponent had been Dominic Monaco, once fifth in the world as a junior lightweight, and a coach at Gleason's. Clearly I had caught him off guard that first time.

He wanted a rematch.

From that day on, training became my life. I plunged back into the steely routine with an odd sense of relief. The fight was a mirage that flickered on the horizon; the training was the real event, and it was as regular as the tide, though constantly enlivened by the unexpected. The third day of training, I arrived to find a bullet-headed guy with banana yellow gloves. He grunted that he was preparing for an Ultimate Fighting match. He was macho, moody, and taciturn. He seemed to have strutted out of a video game.

"What is Ultimate Fighting?" I asked DeWitt after Action Man had departed.

"Grappling," he said dismissively. "I don't know. Any boxer could kill those guys. He didn't have a punch."

DeWitt, like most boxers, feels just as lukewarm about karate, aikido, tai chi, and you-name-the-fight-flavor-of-the-month. Boxing is the Big Beefsteak. The rest is just takeout. Muhammad Ali's farcical fight with a Japanese wrestler came to mind. DeWitt ruminated about Ali's career as he drove me to the train station near his gym. "Muhammad Ali was the most overrated fighter. He had lightning speed, great showmanship. But not much of a punch," he said. "But he had a heart like a lion. Larry Holmes gave him a brutal beating, and he wouldn't go down."

Was it because of Ali's notorious rope-a-dope that he developed Parkinson's?

"Ali's condition is 100 percent on account of his boxing," DeWitt claimed. "He had a strong chin, but he didn't protect it. And he fought some real bangers.

Boxing is as intimate as sweat.

You learn which champs are mean, which are cheap, which are pussy-whipped, which are gay. Secret histories. The lore becomes part of the training. In DeWitt's telling, yesterday's names become shiny bright. "Joe Louis only used the heavy bag. Jake La Motta never used the speed bag. He never ran," he says, adding with relish, "He was a brute. He didn't eat solid food for 5 days before his last fight with Sugar Ray Robinson. To make the weight.

And Sugar is sitting down to these huge steaks. Sugar used to drink blood. They called him 'The Vampire.' "

DeWitt says the greatest fights were in the '80s,"in all the divisions, partly because they used to go 15 rounds." Then came the fight between Ray "Boom-Boom" Mancini and a Korean fighter, Duk Koo Kim. "Kim was outmatched. He said he would die if he didn't win," DeWitt says.

He did die. In the ring. Fifteen-rounders were banned.

One day DeWitt gave me a tour of his Scarsdale apartment. It's hung with trophies, belts, and family portraits. He turned pro at 18. "I put myself in prison for 5 years to become a world-class fighter," he says. "When I was ready, I could fight with anybody. Anybody! But I still trained my ass off. I needed to do that. I wasn't Thomas Hearns—a one-punch- knockout guy, 6-foot-2, a 70-inch reach. I had to win my fights by fighting—outsmarting, outpunching, outgutting. None of my fights were easy."

He popped a video of his fight with Tommy "Hit Man" Hearns into the VCR. Round 6 and Hearns was in trouble. But DeWitt lost. One of several badly timed losses. DeWitt came back to win the middleweight title but lost it in 1990 to the Briton Nigel Benn.

"I took him too lightly," he said. "It was a brutal fight. We both went down. I almost lost my ear." The next boxer who stepped into the ring with Benn is still in a coma.

"I shouldn't even have been in that fight," DeWitt says. "My prime was in the mid-'80s. I f ---ed up. They'll all tell you! 'Doug DeWitt did not fulfill his potential.' I was world champion! I was regarded as a great fighter. But was I a superstar? No. Was I a guy who made these big, multimillion-dollar paydays? No. And I think I should've. There's something inside of me today that . . . " He gave an old-fashioned groan, the kind of groan that was groaned in 19th-century novels. "Could've! Would've! Should've! It angers me. I'm not a failure in boxing. I'm a success. But not to the level I should have been. I was thinking big."

He is now studying acting. "The second most masochistic profession," he observed, and began declaiming the monologue he was working on as we drove to the station. DeWitt was at a crossroads, too.

The next morning, DeWitt wiped me out in training. Brutally. "You pushed me to the limit three times," I told him.

"I could tell," he said. He was nonchalant.

Rounds of sparring began. The match was approaching. Common sense suggested I investigate my opponent's usual moves. "He likes to move in close and land uppercuts," Leslie told me. "He cuffs you with his left hook. He paws you like a cat. He doesn't hit very hard, but it's irritating. I'll show you." We agreed to spar at Gleason's.

Gleason's doesn't have the chill chic of a sports club. There are no black rubberized floors, glimmering white machines, bouncy background vocals. Gleason's is gamy. All you hear at Gleason's are thuds and thwacks.

Dominic Monaco himself popped up, quite suddenly, his face bisected by a gleaming grin. He looked compact. I weighed 148 pounds, and I would guess he was some pounds lighter.

I don't know his exact age. He stopped the sparring to demonstrate the way I should rise from the balls of my feet so as to pack more body weight into a punch.

I followed his instructions. I wasn't sure whether to be encouraged or alarmed by my future opponent's generosity.

"I don't think he's going to want to kill me," I would say if the subject of Monaco came up. But friends showed a talent to unnerve.

"Boxers can't help it," the photographer Harry Benson said. "Sly Stallone sparred with Ernie Shavers before Rocky. Shavers hit him so hard in the solar plexus that it paralyzed him for 6 months." Benson cackled like a madman.

I mentioned the Stallone anecdote to DeWitt. "Yeah, that's right," he said. "And then when Mickey Rourke was sparring with Tommy Hearns, he got cute. Hearns threw a good punch. Rourke was unconscious for 5 minutes." Only Robert DeNiro, who had undergone his customary metamorphosis while preparing for Raging Bull, earned DeWitt's approval. "He was looking really good when he trained," he said. He pointed at my camera. "La Motta told me every time he turned around, DeNiro would be there with one of those things."

The night before the fight, I went to a party. This was not a brilliant move, nor did I cut a dashing figure—my caps had been loosened by the sparring, a bridge had broken. A boxer I knew was in attendance. He spoke gravely.

"Listen to me," he said. "Do not get into the ring. I know this Monaco. He's had a hundred fights. He's fought everybody."

I could not withdraw.

DeWitt and I arrived the next evening at St. Anne's Warehouse, the space where the event would be held. There was a crowd outside. DeWitt oversaw the bandaging of my hands, the greasing up of my face, the fitting of the red leather headgear (which does little to cushion a blow, by the way; but it does prevent cuts). He got me shadowboxing, then suddenly cuffed me hard on the side of the head as a kind of aperitif for what was to come.

I was given a robe of shiny white satin, hemmed in black. DeWitt preceded me as I walked out to the ring. Sound faded to a background thrum. Faces floated. I was in the blue corner. I climbed in. The round began.

Doug DeWitt says he remembers "every fight, every punch" of his career. I remember vivid fragments. I landed a couple of left jabs.

I felt a rush and moved forward. I felt no primal rage, no urge to hurt. Indeed, I felt unnaturally remote. Friends behind the ropes looked vivid, overanimated, like children at a birthday party. A distraction. I broke the connection—it was like putting down the telephone—and focused on my opponent. Then Monaco got me with that pesky left hook. And again. So Leslie had been right.

I landed a right cross. Monaco's face, like that of many club fighters, seemed to squish in and out, like rubber, registering no pain.

I was heaving by the end of the round, but not wrung out. Just heaving.

In the second round, Monaco came on strong and threw a flurry of hard punches to the body. I was buffeted, like a ship in a storm, but it didn't exactly hurt. I don't remember ever feeling pain during a fight. But I have been stabbed, and that didn't hurt, either. (Any stabbing victim will tell you the same, I think.) Of course, bruises, breaks, or cuts will hurt like hell later.

I clocked Monaco with a right hook to the head. He went down for an eight count.

The third round was easier. I felt free of the undertow of fatigue. Monaco went down once more. I had thought it was a slip, but he took another eight count.

Despite that blizzard of body punches, I was declared the winner on points.

I got a trophy and a sense of satisfaction, and I watched Leslie take to the ring against the hulking Cooney. (Leslie would lose, but not by the knockout he was hoping for. He was, however, in la-la land for some days afterward. A concussion. "I got a bruised brain," he told me cheerfully.)

There was only bonhomie amongst the ring people afterward. Writers tend to be guarded around their kind. Artists likewise. But boxers tend to treat other boxers, at whatever level of competence, as people who have . . . been there. "You looked good out there. Nobody's going to tell me you didn't look good," DeWitt told me. Praise makes me uneasy, as though it's tempting fate. Not this time. I have to assume that a boxer of Dominic Monaco's experience could have exacted his revenge if he had pressed, had gone hog-wild. But he hadn't.

At the Box Opera afterparty, I watched a film of the event. I could no more relate to the image of myself boxing than to a space alien in a monster movie, but, yes, the alien looked efficient. Now was clearly the time to prove DeWitt wrong. I should quit while I was ahead, go out on top. Right?

Perhaps. But I'm back in training. And I have tentatively agreed to one more fight. Definitely the last.
