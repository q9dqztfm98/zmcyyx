# Stop Making This One Mistake in Your Overhead Press

In a recent video, YouTuber Eugene Teo broke down a common mistake that people make in their overhead press technique, which can limit their potential in building strength and muscle in their shoulders, and demonstrated how to overcome it.

[Stop Making This One](https://collagen-atelier.bbsxyxy.com/)

Make your range of motion personal

When performing the overhead press, a lot of people tend to bring the bar right down to their chest, so that it's resting on the collarbone, and lift from there. And yes, this is optimal positioning, but it's not necessarily how you are built. "For all intents and purposes, that looks pretty good; my elbows are tracking in a good position, bar path is a straight line, I'm not overextending in my lower back," says Teo. "But when you get heavier, a lot of issues will start to manifest."

As you add more weight to the bar, your movement pattern changes. So yes, when you're just using the weight of the bar, you may think you have the shoulder mobility you need to lower all the way to your clavicle and press back up. But once you're adding plates, don't be surprised if that technique becomes a little less perfect, says Teo. And that's when problems happen.

That's why it's important to maximize the efficiency of the shoulder-press movement pattern, understanding that you don't move like everyone else.

To determine your own safe range of motion, Teo suggests mimicking a pulldown move; once your back and shoulder muscles are engaged at the lower end, you'll come to a natural stop. This is the bottom you should be aiming for. If you continue to lower the bar beyond what is comfortable, your body will start to compensate. This means you might end up leaning to one side, moving your elbows, or arching your back. And when you're starting out each rep from a poor position, it can start to affect the rest of the movement.

"That's where we start to see these compensatory patterns happening long term, and that will limit how much strength you can express, and it might even create shoulder impingements and shoulder pain," he says.

If you do want to exploit that additional range of motion, Teo suggests switching a barbell for dumbbells, adopting a neutral grip, and tucking your elbows slightly forward in order to get the safest, most effective benefits from those extra few inches.

"The barbell is great to use, as long as you know where your personal range is," he explains. "But don't be afraid to use dumbbells, neutral grip bars, and a high incline, depending on your own mobility."
