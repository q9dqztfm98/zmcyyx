# Extended Women's Health Panel: Hormones & More

Test details

What the Extended Women's Health Panel Measures

Fatigue that doesn't lift with sleep. Cycles that have shifted. Hair that isn't as thick as it used to be. Weight that moved without an obvious reason. Mood that feels harder to steady. These experiences are common, and they are often hormonal, yet the markers behind them rarely appear on a standard annual physical. The Extended Women's Health Panel is an add-on that layers 10 women's-health markers on top of your Baseline Blood Panel: reproductive hormones, pituitary signals, thyroid antibodies, and mercury. Steroid hormones such as estradiol, progesterone, and 17-OHP are measured from a single blood sample.

Why this panel includes mercury

Mercury is a documented environmental endocrine disruptor that may interfere with hormone signaling at the cellular level, including thyroid and estrogen pathways. Common sources include large predatory fish such as tuna and swordfish, dental amalgam fillings, and some cosmetic products. Most hormone panels skip it entirely. This one includes it because unexplained hormonal symptoms do not always have a purely hormonal origin. If mercury is elevated, that context is associated with how the rest of your results should be read, and is something to discuss with your care team or provider.

Frequently asked questions

What does AMH tell me?

AMH, or anti-Müllerian hormone, is produced by the follicles in your ovaries and reflects the size of your remaining follicle pool. It's the most reliable single marker available for ovarian reserve. It does not measure egg quality, and it does not predict whether you'll conceive or when you'll reach menopause. Think of it as one data point in a broader planning conversation with your care team.

Does this panel diagnose PCOS?

No. This panel provides hormonal data (LH:FSH context, 17-OHP, AMH, and estradiol) that is relevant to evaluating PCOS patterns, but it does not diagnose PCOS. A PCOS evaluation also involves clinical assessment and imaging by a provider, and these results are most useful reviewed alongside your full clinical picture.

Does this panel confirm perimenopause?

No. Perimenopause is a clinical assessment that considers symptoms, age, and lab patterns over time. Shifting FSH, estradiol, and AMH are associated with the perimenopausal transition, but a single test result is not a diagnosis. This panel gives you objective data to bring to that conversation.

What if my thyroid antibodies come back elevated?

Elevated TPO or thyroglobulin antibodies may indicate autoimmune thyroid activity, even when TSH is normal. This is worth discussing with a provider who can assess whether further thyroid monitoring or evaluation is appropriate for you.

How is this different from a basic hormone panel?

A basic hormone panel often covers estradiol, FSH, LH, and TSH. This add-on layers on additional markers, including AMH, progesterone, prolactin, 17-OHP, thyroid antibodies, and mercury.

Biomarkers tested

17-Hydroxyprogesterone

17-Hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP) is a steroid hormone that acts as an intermediate step in the production of other hormones.

Method: Laboratory-developed test (LDT) validated under CLIA; not cleared or approved by the FDA. Results are interpreted by clinicians in context and are not a stand-alone diagnosis.

Estradiol

Follicle Stimulating Hormone (FSH)

FSH is a hormone produced by the pituitary gland that helps the reproductive system develop eggs in women and sperm in men.

Method: FDA-cleared clinical laboratory assay performed in CLIA-certified, CAP-accredited laboratories. Used to aid clinician-directed evaluation and monitoring. Not a stand-alone diagnosis.

Luteinizing hormone (LH)

LH stands for luteinizing hormone, which is a hormone produced in the anterior pituitary gland involved in regulation of the reproductive function.

Mercury, Blood

Mercury is a naturally occurring heavy metal with no beneficial role in human biology.

Anti-Mullerian Hormone

Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) is a protein hormone produced by the small, developing follicles in a woman’s ovaries.
