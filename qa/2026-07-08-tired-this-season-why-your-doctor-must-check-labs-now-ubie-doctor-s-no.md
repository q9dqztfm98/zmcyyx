Explanation

Many people notice feeling sleepier once cold weather sets in and daylight hours shorten. While it's normal to adjust your routines during fall and winter, persistent sleepiness and low energy can signal underlying issues. Here's why your doctor may order laboratory tests when you complain of sleepiness in cold weather—and what you can do.

Why Cold Weather and Short Days Make You Sleepier

Several factors contribute to increased tiredness as temperatures drop:

Melatonin shifts

Melatonin is the hormone that helps you fall asleep.

Shorter days and longer nights boost melatonin production earlier in the afternoon, making you feel drowsy sooner.

Circadian rhythm changes

Your internal clock is guided by daylight.

Reduced light in winter can shift your body's schedule, leading to earlier sleepiness and groggy mornings.

Temperature effects

Cooler temperatures signal your body to slow down, conserving energy.

Without proper insulation or warm bedding, your sleep quality can suffer.

Vitamin D deficiency

Sunlight fuels vitamin D production, which supports mood and energy.

Low vitamin D levels are linked to fatigue, especially in regions with limited winter sun.

How Seasonal Sleepiness Can Mask Health Issues

Doctors pay close attention when sleepiness seems out of proportion to seasonal changes. These lab tests help rule out or confirm conditions that mimic "winter tiredness":

Thyroid Function Tests

Why It Matters: An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) slows your metabolism, causing fatigue, weight gain, and cold sensitivity.

What's Checked: TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), Free T4, Free T3.

Complete Blood Count (CBC)

Why It Matters: Anemia, often due to low iron or vitamin B12, reduces oxygen delivery to tissues, leaving you exhausted.

What's Checked: Hemoglobin, hematocrit, red blood cell indices.

Vitamin D Level

Why It Matters: Vitamin D supports muscle strength, immune function, and mood regulation. Deficiency can intensify winter tiredness.

What's Checked: 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration.

Metabolic Panel

Why It Matters: Imbalances in blood glucose, electrolytes, or kidney and liver function can present as fatigue.

What's Checked: Blood sugar, sodium, potassium, creatinine, liver enzymes.

Inflammatory Markers

Why It Matters: Chronic inflammation from autoimmune diseases or infections can sap energy.

What's Checked: C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).

Hormone Levels

Why It Matters: Adrenal insufficiency or low testosterone/estrogen may cause lethargy and mood changes.

What's Checked: Cortisol, sex hormones as indicated.

Sleep Apnea Evaluation (Indirect)

Why It Matters: Obstructive sleep apnea often worsens in winter if weight increases or nasal congestion rises.

What's Checked: Overnight oximetry, referral for a sleep study if needed.

What Your Doctor Looks For

After lab results come back, your physician will:

Compare results to seasonal expectations

Identify treatable deficiencies (e.g., iron, vitamin D, thyroid)

Screen for chronic diseases that require management

Suggest lifestyle adjustments to sync your rhythm with daylight

Coordinate referrals (e.g., sleep clinic, endocrinologist)

Final Thoughts

Seasonal sleep changes and cold-weather sleepiness are common, but they shouldn't be shrugged off if they start to interfere with your well-being.
