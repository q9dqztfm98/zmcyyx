Why Your Heel Won't Heal: PRP for Plantar Fasciitis and Next Steps

Plantar fasciitis is one of the most common causes of chronic heel pain. In most cases, conservative treatments (rest, ice, stretching, physical therapy) help resolve symptoms within a few months. But for 10–20% of people, heel pain lingers beyond six months, impacting daily activities and quality of life. If you're one of them, understanding why your heel won't heal—and whether platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy could help—is key to getting back on your feet.

What Is Plantar Fasciitis and Why Doesn't It Heal? Plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue running from your heel bone (calcaneus) to the base of your toes. It supports the arch of your foot and absorbs shock. Overuse, foot mechanics, obesity or tight calf muscles can overload this structure, causing microtears and inflammation. Chronic cases develop when:

• Ongoing stress outpaces the tissue's natural repair capacity• Scar tissue builds up, creating stiffness and pain• Biomechanical factors (high arches, flat feet, overpronation) continue to strain the fascia• You return to activity too quickly without adequate rehab

Even after the initial inflammation settles, scarred or poorly healed tissue can remain a pain generator. That's why some heels just "never get better" without targeted intervention.

What Is PRP and How Does It Work? Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous blood-derived treatment. Here's the basic process:

Blood Draw

Injection

PRP is injected directly into the damaged tissue under ultrasound guidance

Growth factors in PRP (VEGF, PDGF, TGF-β, IGF-1) may:

• Enhance cell proliferation and migration• Promote new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis)• Modulate inflammation• Stimulate collagen synthesis and tissue remodeling

Evidence for PRP in Plantar Fasciitis Several peer-reviewed studies and meta-analyses have evaluated PRP for chronic plantar fasciitis:

• A 2018 randomized trial showed greater pain reduction and functional improvement at 6 months with PRP vs. corticosteroid injections.• A systematic review in 2021 reported PRP led to sustained pain relief up to 12 months, with fewer complications than steroids.• Comparative studies suggest PRP may be as effective as extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT), with longer-lasting benefits.

Overall, PRP appears most beneficial when:

• Pain has lasted longer than 6 months despite conservative care• Ultrasound shows plantar fascia thickening (>4 mm) or partial tears• You prefer a therapy free of steroids or systemic side effects

Who Is a Good Candidate for PRP? Consider PRP if you:

• Have chronic heel pain (≥6–12 months) unresponsive to conservative measures• Are in generally good health, without bleeding disorders or active infections• Understand that multiple injections (1–3) may be needed, spaced 4–6 weeks apart• Can commit to post-injection rehabilitation

PRP may not be appropriate if you:

• Have advanced degenerative changes in the heel bone or ankle joint• Are on anticoagulant therapy that can't be safely paused• Have uncontrolled diabetes or autoimmune conditions that impair healing

What to Expect During and After PRP Treatment Procedure:

Preparation

Local anesthetic to numb the area

Ultrasound guidance to target the exact site of pathology

PRP is slowly injected into the plantar fascia origin at the heel

Post-Procedure

Brief observation (10–15 minutes)

Optional light bandage; avoid strenuous activity

Rehabilitation Timeline:

• Days 1–3: Rest, ice, foot elevation• Week 1: Gentle stretching, non-weight-bearing activities• Weeks 2–4: Gradual return to weight-bearing, physical therapy begins• Months 2–3: Progressive strengthening, return to low-impact cardio• Months 3–6: Resume most activities, guided by pain levels

Recovery varies by individual, but many see meaningful improvement in 8–12 weeks. Full benefits may take up to 6 months.

Risks and Considerations PRP is generally safe, as it uses your own blood, but potential risks include:

• Temporary increased pain or swelling at the injection site• Infection (rare with proper sterile technique)• Nerve irritation or damage (minimized with ultrasound guidance)

Discuss these risks and your medical history with a qualified provider before proceeding.

When to Seek Further Medical Attention Heel pain that is severe, worsening rapidly, or accompanied by:

• Redness, warmth, or fever (signs of infection)• Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the foot• Inability to bear weight despite rest

may indicate more serious conditions (stress fracture, nerve entrapment, arthritis) and warrants prompt evaluation. Next Steps and When to Talk to a Doctor

Review your history of conservative treatments.

Get a focused exam and ultrasound if you haven't already.

Discuss PRP candidacy, costs, and expected outcomes with a sports medicine or foot-and-ankle specialist.

Commit to post-PRP rehabilitation to maximize healing.

If you experience anything life-threatening or serious—such as signs of infection, sudden severe pain, or loss of function—seek immediate medical care or call emergency services. Otherwise, schedule an appointment with your doctor to determine the best personalized strategy for healing your heel.

Remember: a persistent heel that won't heal often responds when the right diagnosis and targeted treatment come together. Whether that's PRP for plantar fasciitis or another advanced therapy, your next step is to get a thorough assessment and speak to a qualified physician.
