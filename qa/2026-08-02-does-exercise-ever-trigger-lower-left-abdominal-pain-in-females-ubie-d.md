# Does exercise ever trigger lower left abdominal pain in females? | Ubie Doctor's Note

Does exercise ever trigger lower left abdominal pain in females?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Exercise can cause lower-left abdominal pain in females, most often from gastrointestinal issues like side stitches, gas, bloating, or IBS, as well as muscle strains. Common triggers include poor meal timing, dehydration, and sudden increases in workout intensity. However, lower-left abdominal pain during exercise can also indicate more serious conditions, including gynecologic problems (ovarian cysts or, rarely, ovarian torsion), urinary tract infections, kidney stones, diverticulitis, or hernias. Seek urgent care for sudden severe pain, fever, vomiting, blood in urine or stool, or a growing groin bulge.

Explanation

Does Exercise Trigger Lower Left Abdominal Pain in Females?

Many women notice a nagging pain in the lower left side of the abdomen during or after exercise. While this can feel alarming, there are several common and generally benign explanations—as well as a few serious causes that require prompt medical attention. Below, we'll review possible triggers, prevention strategies, and guidelines for when to see a doctor.

1. Exercise-Induced Gastrointestinal Causes

Exercise, especially high-intensity or long-duration workouts, can lead to lower left abdominal discomfort. According to Costa et al. (2017), exercise-induced gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms arise from:

Splanchnic hypoperfusion: Blood flow is shunted to active muscles, which can disrupt digestion.

Mechanical jostling: Repetitive bouncing (e.g., running) can irritate the intestines.

Neuroendocrine changes: Stress hormones alter gut motility and sensitivity.

Common GI triggers include:

Periodic "side stitches": Usually felt in the lower abdomen or just below the ribs; often resolves if you slow down, exhale fully, or press gently into the painful area.

Gas and bloating: Exercise can speed up transit, causing trapped gas in the left colon (splenic flexure).

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) flare-ups: Triggered by stress, dehydration, or certain sports drinks.

Tips to reduce GI pain:

Eat a light meal 2–3 hours before exercising.

Stay hydrated—small sips of water rather than large gulps.

Avoid high-fiber or gas-producing foods (beans, broccoli) before workouts.

Warm up and cool down gradually.

2. Musculoskeletal Causes

Strains or overuse injuries of the abdominal wall and nearby muscles can mimic internal pain:

Oblique muscle strain• Occurs with twisting motions (e.g., golf swings, tennis serves).• Pain worsens with trunk rotation or side-bending.

Rectus abdominis (six-pack) strain• Common in core-intensive workouts (planks, crunches).• Pain is localized, tender to palpation, and increases with sit-up movements.

Iliopsoas bursitis or strain• Felt deep in the lower abdomen, often radiating to the groin.• Triggered by lunges, squats, or heavy lifting.

Preventive measures:

Warm up with dynamic stretches (side bends, hip circles).

Strengthen core musculature evenly—include both oblique and posterior (back) exercises.

Use proper form and avoid sudden increases in intensity.

3. Gynecologic Causes

Women have unique reproductive-system issues that may present as left-sided pain during exercise:

Ovarian cysts• Fluid-filled sacs on the ovary; can cause a dull ache or sharp twinge.• Pain may worsen with high-impact movements.

Ovarian torsion (rare but serious)• Twisting of the ovary cutting off blood flow.• Sudden, severe pain often accompanied by nausea; medical emergency.

Endometriosis• Endometrial tissue growing outside the uterus; may ache or cramp during exercise.• Pain often correlates with menstrual cycle but can flare with exertion.

Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)• Infection of reproductive organs; can cause constant or intermittent pain.• May be accompanied by fever or unusual discharge.

When to worry:

Sudden, severe pain or pain with fever.

Nausea/vomiting or inability to continue exercising.

Irregular bleeding or signs of infection.

4. Urologic and Renal Causes

Although less common, urinary tract issues can manifest as lower abdominal pain:

Kidney stones• Sharp, cramping pain that may radiate to the groin.• Often worse with movement; may have blood in the urine.

Urinary tract infections (UTIs)• Burning with urination, frequent urges, plus lower-abdominal ache.

Hydration and urine testing can help distinguish these causes. Always seek medical care if you notice blood in your urine or develop fever/chills.

5. Bowel and Colonic Conditions

Certain colonic conditions localize to the lower left quadrant:

Diverticulitis• Inflammation of diverticula (small pouches) in the colon.• Manifests as constant, deep pain—often worsened by coughing or straining.• May be accompanied by fever, nausea, or changes in bowel habits.

Constipation• Hard stools can stretch the colon, causing discomfort when running or jumping.

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)• Ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease can cause cramping, urgency, or diarrhea.

If you have persistent changes in bowel habits, rectal bleeding, or significant fevers, seek care promptly.

6. Vascular and Hernia-Related Causes

Inguinal hernia• Protrusion of abdominal tissue through a weakened spot in the groin area.• Pain or bulge that increases with lifting, coughing, or straining.

Sports hernia (athletic pubalgia)• Strain of the lower abdominal wall; common in soccer, hockey.• Sharp groin pain, aggravated by twisting or sprinting.

Hernias often require surgical consultation, especially if you notice a growing bulge or severe pain.

Prevention Strategies

Alternate high-impact days (running) with low-impact (swimming, cycling).

When to See a Doctor

While many causes of exercise-related lower left abdominal pain are benign and self-limiting, you should speak to a doctor if you experience:

Sudden, severe pain that prevents you from exercising

Fever, chills, or signs of infection

In rare cases, lower left-side pain can signal life-threatening conditions like ovarian torsion or severe diverticulitis.
