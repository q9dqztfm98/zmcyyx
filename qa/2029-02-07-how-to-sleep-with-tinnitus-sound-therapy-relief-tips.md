Why tinnitus gets louder at night

The brain amplifies what it cannot ignore

Tinnitus is not actually getting louder at night. Your perception of it changes. During the day, competing sounds from conversation, traffic, music, and ambient noise partially mask the ringing. When those sounds disappear at bedtime, your brain's auditory cortex has nothing else to process. It turns up the gain on the only signal available: your tinnitus.

Researchers call this auditory gain. When external auditory input decreases, the brain has been shown to compensate by increasing neural activity in the auditory cortex. That amplification makes tinnitus feel more intense and intrusive.

Stress and fatigue compound the problem

By the end of the day, your stress response has been active for hours. Elevated cortisol and adrenaline heighten sensory perception, including the perception of tinnitus. Fatigue also lowers your threshold for emotional reactivity, making the ringing feel more distressing. You are not just hearing it more clearly. You are reacting to it more strongly.

This emotional response matters because sleep anxiety feeds into the cycle. You start dreading bedtime, which raises your stress further, which makes the tinnitus louder. Breaking that loop is the foundation of most tinnitus sleep strategies.

Sound therapy for tinnitus at bedtime

White noise, pink noise, and nature sounds

Sound masking is the single most effective immediate strategy for learning how to sleep with tinnitus. The goal is not to drown out the ringing but to reduce the contrast between tinnitus and silence. When your brain has other sounds to process, it deprioritizes the tinnitus signal.

White noise: Equal energy across all frequencies. Works well for high-pitched tinnitus because it fills the frequency range where ringing typically sits.

Pink noise: More energy in lower frequencies, creating a deeper, softer sound. Many people find it more natural and less fatiguing than white noise. White and pink noise both improve sleep onset for tinnitus sufferers.

Nature sounds: Rain, ocean waves, and flowing water provide gentle, variable masking. The natural variation keeps the sound from becoming predictable, which helps prevent habituation.

How to choose the right volume

Set your sound source just below the level of your tinnitus. You should still be able to hear the ringing faintly. This partial masking approach, called the mixing point, trains your brain to classify the tinnitus as background noise rather than a threat signal. Over time, this reduces the emotional charge associated with the sound.

How to sleep with tinnitus using relaxation techniques

Progressive muscle relaxation

Starting at your toes and working upward, tense each muscle group for five seconds, then release. The contrast between tension and relaxation teaches your body to let go of the physical guarding that tinnitus triggers. Progressive muscle relaxation has been found to significantly reduce tinnitus distress and improve sleep quality in participants who practiced nightly.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)

CBT-I is the gold standard treatment for chronic insomnia, and it is especially effective for tinnitus-related sleep problems. It retrains the thought patterns that link tinnitus to anxiety and wakefulness. Instead of lying in bed thinking "I will never fall asleep with this noise," CBT-I helps you reframe that thought into something less charged.

A randomized trial has found that CBT-I delivered significant improvements in sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency, and tinnitus distress. Many therapists now offer it online, making it accessible even if you cannot find a local specialist.

Diaphragmatic breathing before bed

Slow, deep belly breathing activates your parasympathetic nervous system and directly counters the fight-or-flight response that tinnitus can trigger. Breathe in for four counts, hold for two, and exhale for six. Practice for five to ten minutes in bed. This technique also helps people who experience a racing heart at night alongside tinnitus.

Optimizing your sleep environment

Keep the room cool and dark

A bedroom temperature between 65 and 68 degrees Fahrenheit supports melatonin production and deep sleep. Blackout curtains eliminate light pollution that can further delay sleep onset. The goal is to remove every obstacle to sleep except the one you are already addressing with sound therapy.

Consistent sleep schedule

Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day strengthens your circadian rhythm, which directly affects how quickly you fall asleep. Irregular schedules weaken this rhythm and make it easier for tinnitus to dominate your attention during the vulnerable window of sleep onset.

If you feel tired but cannot sleep, avoid staying in bed for more than 20 minutes. Get up, move to a dim room, and do a quiet activity until drowsiness returns. This prevents your brain from associating the bed with wakefulness and tinnitus frustration.

Limit caffeine and stimulants

Caffeine can increase tinnitus perception in some people by stimulating the central nervous system. Even if caffeine does not directly worsen your tinnitus, it delays sleep onset and reduces sleep depth. Cut off caffeine by early afternoon. Nicotine is another stimulant that can both worsen tinnitus and disrupt sleep architecture.

Can tinnitus cause sleep apnea?

Shared risk factors, not direct causation

Tinnitus does not directly cause sleep apnea, but the two conditions share significant overlap. Tinnitus patients have been found to have a higher prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea compared to the general population. The likely connection runs through shared risk factors: aging, cardiovascular changes, obesity, and chronic inflammation.

How sleep apnea may worsen tinnitus

Obstructive sleep apnea causes repeated oxygen drops during sleep, which can damage the delicate hair cells in the inner ear. This damage is one of the most common causes of tinnitus. Treating sleep apnea with CPAP therapy has been shown to reduce tinnitus severity in some patients, suggesting that improving oxygenation may protect auditory function.

If you snore heavily, wake gasping, or experience daytime dizziness alongside tinnitus, ask your doctor about a sleep study. Diagnosing and treating sleep apnea could improve both your sleep and your tinnitus.

Can lack of sleep cause tinnitus?

Sleep deprivation amplifies the signal

Can lack of sleep cause tinnitus? Research suggests it can worsen existing tinnitus and may even trigger new episodes. Sleep-deprived participants have reported significantly louder tinnitus perception compared to when they were well-rested. Sleep deprivation increases neural excitability in the auditory cortex, essentially turning up the volume knob on phantom sounds.

The vicious cycle of tinnitus and insomnia

Poor sleep makes tinnitus louder. Louder tinnitus makes sleep harder. This feedback loop is one of the most frustrating aspects of living with tinnitus. Breaking the cycle usually requires addressing both sides simultaneously: sound therapy to reduce nighttime tinnitus perception and sleep hygiene or CBT-I to rebuild healthy sleep patterns.

Tracking your deep sleep duration can reveal whether your interventions are working. If you are consistently missing restorative sleep stages, your tinnitus perception is likely to remain elevated regardless of other treatments.

When to see a specialist

Red flags that need evaluation

Most tinnitus is bilateral and linked to noise exposure or aging. But certain patterns warrant prompt evaluation:

Tinnitus in only one ear (may indicate acoustic neuroma or vascular issue)

Pulsatile tinnitus that matches your heartbeat (suggests a vascular cause)

Sudden onset tinnitus accompanied by hearing loss

Tinnitus with vertigo or balance problems (may indicate Meniere's disease)

Tinnitus that significantly worsens over weeks despite intervention

Audiologist vs. ENT

An audiologist can perform a comprehensive hearing evaluation and tinnitus assessment, including matching the pitch and loudness of your tinnitus. They also fit hearing aids, which can provide both amplification and built-in sound therapy. An ENT (ear, nose, and throat) specialist investigates underlying medical causes and can order imaging if needed. Many tinnitus patients benefit from seeing both.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best sound to sleep with for tinnitus?

White noise and pink noise are the most effective for tinnitus masking at bedtime. White noise covers high-pitched ringing well, while pink noise sounds softer and more natural. Nature sounds like rain or ocean waves also work. Set the volume just below your tinnitus level for the best results.

Can tinnitus cause sleep apnea?

Tinnitus does not directly cause sleep apnea, but the two conditions frequently coexist. They share risk factors including aging, cardiovascular changes, and chronic inflammation. Treating sleep apnea with CPAP therapy may reduce tinnitus severity by improving nighttime oxygenation to the inner ear.

Can lack of sleep cause tinnitus?

Sleep deprivation can worsen existing tinnitus and may trigger new episodes. Poor sleep increases neural excitability in the auditory cortex, amplifying phantom sound perception. Restoring healthy sleep patterns through CBT-I and sound therapy often reduces tinnitus loudness and distress.

Should I use earplugs for tinnitus at night?

Earplugs generally make tinnitus worse at night because they eliminate ambient sounds, increasing the contrast between silence and ringing. Pillow speakers or bedside sound machines are better alternatives. They add sound to your environment rather than subtracting it.

Does melatonin help tinnitus?

Some small studies suggest melatonin may reduce tinnitus severity, particularly in people whose tinnitus disrupts sleep. A study in the Annals of Otology found that 3 mg of melatonin at bedtime improved tinnitus scores and sleep quality. However, evidence remains preliminary. Discuss melatonin with your doctor.

Can hearing aids help with tinnitus at night?

Modern hearing aids with built-in sound generators can mask tinnitus during the day. At night, most people remove their hearing aids, so the masking stops. Using a separate bedside sound machine at night provides consistent coverage throughout sleep.

How long does it take to get used to sleeping with tinnitus?

Habituation varies widely. With consistent sound therapy and relaxation techniques, many people notice improvement within four to eight weeks. CBT-I programs typically run six to eight sessions and show measurable sleep improvement by the end of treatment.

Does anxiety make tinnitus worse at night?

Yes. Anxiety activates the amygdala, which amplifies threat perception, including the perception of tinnitus. The quiet of nighttime removes distractions that buffer anxiety during the day. Relaxation techniques like progressive muscle relaxation and diaphragmatic breathing directly counter this feedback loop.
