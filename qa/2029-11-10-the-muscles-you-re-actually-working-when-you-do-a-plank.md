# The Muscles You're Actually Working When You Do a Plank

[Actually Working When You](https://pepperedx.com/5v3mml6cke/)

THERE'S A REASON people often refer to strength training simply as "lifting:" it almost always involves movement. Whether you’re heaving iron or hefting your own bodyweight, nearly every exercise you do requires some form of pushing, pulling, hinging, lunging, squatting, or carrying (i.e., one of the six basic movement patterns). But that’s not the only way to build strength and muscular endurance. Indeed, it’s sometimes not even the best way. If you know what you’re doing, you can reach certain goals faster by taking the exact opposite tact: holding still.

Exhibit one: the plank. In its most basic form, the plank is exceedingly straightforward—just assume a pushup position with your arms straight or forearms on the floor and hold that posture for the prescribed amount of time (or for as long as you can before failure). But despite its simplicity, the plank can help you build core strength more quickly than most other abdominal exercises—especially those that involve movement, like the crunch—according to a study in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research.

The reason is simple: When you do a plank, your muscles don’t get a break. There are no reps, no top or bottom of the movement, none of the fleeting relief that comes from transitioning from contraction to elongation, and vice versa. There is only constant contraction on a head-to-toe scale that must be held until you can hold it no more.

As a result, few other exercises can compare to the plank when it comes to muscular activation and time under tension (the undisputed king of muscle-building stimuli). But like every other exercise, you need to execute the plank with proper form to maximize its benefits and ensure that you target the right muscles in the most effective way possible.

What Muscles the Plank Works

No matter what variation of the plank you do, you’re going to engage your entire core—that armada of muscles spanning your torso from top to bottom—and force it to perform its two primary jobs: supporting your spine and resisting any movement of it. But you’ll feel the greatest engagement in these muscles, if you're doing it right:

Rectus Abdominis

External Obliques

The muscular activation doesn’t stop there. In order to prevent your hips from sagging—a key point of proper form—you must engage another core muscle group that most people associate with the lower body: your glutes. Similarly, the muscles in your neck, shoulders, arms, upper back, and legs will be called into action to keep your posture rigid and your body straight from head to heels.

The moment any of them fail and your form falters, the clock stops and the set is over. That’s why most trainers refer to the plank not only as a core move, but also as a total body exercise. That's also why you shouldn't be looking to set records for how long you can hold a plank. Once you lose the ability to keep the tension consistent throughout, you're not getting the benefits. For most people, this will only be around a minute to 90 seconds, tops.

The Benefits of Planks

[Working When](https://tiancansz.com/4zlwmw70v/)

There are many aesthetic dividends to adding the plank to your training program, including a flatter stomach, leaner profile, and more defined abs. But these are just the tip of the iceberg of benefits you’ll ultimately reap.

You’ll also notice that your performance in just about every other exercise improves, for example. That's because the key to the plank is bracing, one of the core's key functions. You're essentially stabilizing your midsection by creating intra-abdominal pressure, and holding this isometric contraction. The stronger and more stable your core is—two direct advantages of performing planks regularly—the greater your ability will be to not only generate force and transfer it throughout your body, but also to apply that force both in the gym (e.g., to a barbell, kettlebell, or resistance band) and in the outside world (e.g., to a couch, bag of concrete mix, etc.). Plus, by challenging your core and helping it become more effective at supporting your spine, you can also reduce your risk of experiencing low back pain, according to a study in the International Journal of Science and Research.

In short, there’s almost no reason not to include the plank in your exercise arsenal. Read on to learn how to perform it perfectly and optimize your results.

How to Do a Plank

Doing the plank is simple—you don't need any equipment, and once you're in position, you won't move—but you won't get the most out of the exercise unless you're a stickler for proper positioning and form. Follow these cues and remember that maintaining tension is key.

Get down on the ground. Stack your elbows directly beneath your shoulders and extend your legs. Rest your weight on your elbows and your toes.

Squeeze your abs and glutes to create full-body tension. Think about pulling your belly button into your spine.

[The Muscles](https://repair-cascade.jcesqc8.com/)

Contract your low back, lats, and rhomboids. Your back should form a straight line; don't let your pelvis dip down or your butt to rise up.

Face your gaze face down, which keeps your neck in a neutral position.

Hold this position, continuing to squeeze your abs and glutes throughout. Once you lose tension, take a break.

How to Add the Plank to Your Workouts

Since the plank is such a low-impact exercise, there's no limit to how often you can include it in your training. Working on bracing will help with your other movements, like deadlifts and squats, so there are more reasons to do it than just rounding out your ab circuit.

To start, perform four rounds of holds for 30 seconds up to a minute. Just make sure you can hold with perfect form, and let that dictate how long you hold. Once you feel confident in your plank, you can try different variations of the exercise, which will level up the exercise and shift the focus to different muscles of the core.
